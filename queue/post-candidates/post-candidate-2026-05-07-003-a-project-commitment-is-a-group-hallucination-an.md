## Draft

A project deadline is a group hallucination.

Everyone in the room knows it's probably wrong. Nobody says so. We call this a plan.

Here's what's actually happening: commitment and consistency are powerful psychological forces. Once you've said a thing out loud — especially in front of your boss — you feel pressure to act as if it's true. Even when the uncertainty is obvious. Even when the estimate was made up.

So the team commits. The boss accepts. Everyone walks out of the room pretending the software already exists in the future exactly as described.

The book I've been writing has a name for this: a foolish commitment. Not malicious. Not stupid. Just — made in conditions that make reliability impossible.

Software projects are shaped by uncertainty, discovery, complexity, and things nobody knows yet. Rigid commitments don't tame that uncertainty. They just delay the conversation about it until the conversation is much more expensive.

The alternative isn't no commitment. It's treating the commitment as the *start* of a negotiation — not the end of one. What really matters here? What's the valuable core? What are the black swans hiding inside this plan?

Ask those questions before the deadline. Not after.
