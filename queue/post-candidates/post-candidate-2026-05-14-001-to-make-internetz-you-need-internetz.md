I worked on a project that cost tens of millions of pounds. The developers couldn't do their work.

Not because the problem was hard. Because the WiFi kept dropping.

This went on for months.

Eventually an exasperated developer lost his cool and tried to explain it to a programme manager.

"To make internetz, you need internetz."

I've thought about that sentence a lot since then. Not because it's funny — though it is — but because of what it means that it had to be said at all. Months in. On a project worth tens of millions of pounds. To a programme manager.

I was in those meetings. I didn't say it like that either.

There's a name for this. I call it agreed activity. Everyone in the room knows the real problem exists. Nobody says it out loud. Instead, you find something you can talk about — something with a clear answer, a budget code, an owner, a resolution. You discuss it until the hour is up.

The real problem is still there when you leave the room.

---

But why? Why do intelligent people — people who can clearly see what's wrong — choose to stay silent?

The answer isn't stupidity. It isn't cowardice, exactly.

It's status. Status isn't something you have. It's something you perform, continuously, in front of other people. And the performance of status makes certain things unsayable.

Raising the real problem risks looking like you don't have a solution. It risks looking like a troublemaker. It risks threatening the credibility of the person who sponsored the project — who may also be your boss, or your boss's boss.

So nobody fixes the WiFi.

---

Here's the thing. This isn't an unusual story. Every large software project I've ever worked on has a version of it.

Sometimes it's the WiFi. Sometimes it's the wrong kind of meetings — stand-ups that don't surface problems, retrospectives that don't lead to change, show-and-tells that show only the good news. Sometimes it's what I call project management theater: the methodologies, the credentials, the ceremonies that make a project look controlled without actually making it controllable.

What all of these have in common: people working very hard on things that are not the problem.

---

Most people who've worked in software recognise this. They've seen the obvious threat on the horizon that everyone is pretending isn't there. They've watched a project drift toward the rocks in slow motion and known, weeks or months in advance, exactly what was going to happen.

Bad news isn't the threat. Bad news is data. The earlier you get it, the more options you have. The later you get it — once it's arrived disguised as a crisis, once all the options have closed — the less you can do.

---

There's a harder version of this, though. One I find more unsettling.

Every project starts as an idea. The idea gets pitched, gets excited people behind it, gets funded. That funding moment is when the project becomes real — when it stops being a possibility and starts being a promise.

But the idea and the product are not the same thing. They never are. The idea lives in the pitch deck, in the sponsor's head, in the emotional logic of why this project had to happen. The product has to be built in the real world, with real users, under real constraints.

In my experience, the gap between those two is where most projects actually fail. Not because the team couldn't build it. Because nobody found a way to say: the idea has changed. The reality is different from the promise. And if we keep pretending otherwise, nobody fixes the WiFi.

---

*Delivering the Impossible* (Apress) is my attempt to give people the language for that conversation.

Not a new methodology. Not a better framework. A way of seeing what's actually happening on your project — the dynamics that textbooks don't cover and that no one discusses in the stand-up.

Every project is a bet. All projects are swamps. Bad news is data. These aren't slogans. They're tools for thinking more clearly about what's in front of you, so you can stop delivering the fantasy and start delivering the possible.

If you work in software — as a developer, a manager, a CTO, or the person who keeps getting handed impossible projects and told to make them work — this is the book I wish I'd had thirty years ago.
