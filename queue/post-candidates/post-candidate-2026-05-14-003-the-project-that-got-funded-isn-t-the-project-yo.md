## Draft

Before your software project started, it won a different kind of competition — one that had almost nothing to do with whether it would actually work.

Somebody had an idea. The idea got attention. It got support. It got funding. And at that moment, the idea became real — in people's minds, at least.

This is the first value stream: the **idea stream**. Ideas flow through it for emotional and political reasons, not because they've been tested. The idea that sounds exciting, that solves a problem the boss feels personally, that can be told as a story — that's the idea that gets funded.

Then the project starts. And now you need to operate in a completely different stream: the **product stream**. Users who don't behave the way the idea assumed. Technical constraints that weren't in the pitch. Scope that keeps expanding because the original specification was really just a description of a feeling.

Here's the problem: the people who championed the idea have a stake in it. Changing it can feel like telling them they were wrong. So teams get pressured to build the fantasy rather than discover the reality.

Successful delivery means navigating the gap between these two streams. It means learning what users actually need, changing the original idea accordingly, and doing it in a way that doesn't feel like a betrayal.

That gap is where most projects fail. Not because teams aren't skilled. Because nobody acknowledged the two streams were different things in the first place.

Deliver the possible, not the fantasy. The fantasy already had its moment — it got the project funded. Now it's your job to build something that actually works.
