The 25 to 30 hours figure is right, and most plans still quietly assume 40. That correction is worth making on its own.

I want to argue with one word. Period.

"If it doesn't fit, it doesn't go in the sprint. Period." That word is carrying the entire method. It names an authority to refuse work, and the people who most need this post do not have it. Anyone who can say Period in that meeting was never overcommitted in the first place. They would have said it last quarter.

Because the commitment usually is not created in the planning meeting. It arrives at the planning meeting. Someone sold it, or it went in a board pack, or it is a date in a contract with a penalty hanging off it. Capacity-aware planning is a genuinely good instrument for discovering that the work does not fit. It says nothing at all about what to do when it does not fit and that answer is not accepted, which is the situation almost everyone reading it is actually in.

Then there is the list of things that go wrong. Someone gets sick. A client emergency. The vendor misses a date.

You have written those as interruptions to the plan. They turn up on every project I have ever worked on. That is not variance around the plan, that is the ground you are standing on. All projects are swamps, and 20 percent is what you budget for an exception, not for the terrain.

The last part is the one I would actually worry about. Teams hit their commitments. Morale improves. Leadership can predict outcomes.

A team can also hit its number every single sprint by quietly committing to a bit less than it can do, and from upstream those two things look identical. Both of them are green. Bad news is data, and a plan that has stopped producing any has not become safe. It has gone quiet.

Predictability is easy to buy. It is worth knowing what you paid for it.
