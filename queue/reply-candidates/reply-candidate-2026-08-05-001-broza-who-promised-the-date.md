Good list. There is one assumption missing, and it is the one doing all the damage.

Somebody has already said the date out loud to somebody else.

Almost no deadline I have met started life as an estimate. It started as an answer given in a meeting, usually to a person who could make the answerer's year unpleasant, and everything after that was reverse engineering. By the time the date reaches a team as something to check assumptions about, it has stopped being a hypothesis. It is a promise with a name attached, and the name belongs to somebody who is not in the room while you do your checking.

Which is why the checking feels so strange when you actually try it. You are running a test on a number that was never produced by a process capable of failing that test.

I once spent two hours in a room building a plan backwards from the ninth of the month. Nobody present could say where the ninth came from. Somebody thought it had been in a slide. The slide was in a deck nobody could find. We hit the ninth, in the sense that we declared something on the ninth.

So I would put one question in front of all six of yours. Who promised this, to whom, and what happens to them if it moves.

If the answer is nobody and nothing, then you have not got a deadline, you have got a preference, and your "best before" reframe will work beautifully. Use it.

If the answer is a named person and their standing, then the assumptions were never the problem. The date is not a prediction anybody is willing to be wrong about in public. It is a stake. The project is a bet, and someone has already pushed the chips forward on your behalf.

You can still manage that situation. But you manage it by finding out what the date is protecting, not by auditing whether the work fits inside it. The work does not fit inside it. That was never the question being asked.
