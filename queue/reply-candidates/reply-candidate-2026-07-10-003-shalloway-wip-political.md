He distinguishes deliberate, controlled task-switching (which can be efficient) from forced task-switching caused by interruption, which signals that the team is working on too many things. He argues the real cost is not the roughly 20 percent efficiency loss to the individual but the delay to the workflow, to feedback, and to delivering value, which costs several times more. His remedy is to address the root cause and reduce the volume of simultaneous work.

Agreed on the diagnosis. The interesting question is the one sitting underneath it, which is who decided the team would work on too many things.

Nobody did. That's what makes it so hard to undo. It arrives as the sum of individually reasonable yeses, each one said by somebody who couldn't afford to say no, none of whom were ever in the same room. There is no meeting at which an organisation resolves to wreck its own throughput.

Which is why a WIP limit is a technical fix for a political problem. The limit holds only if someone has the authority to refuse the next piece of work, and the person closest to the pain is reliably the person with the least authority to refuse anything. Tell them to reduce work in progress and you have handed them a rule they cannot enforce and a fresh way to be at fault.

The interruption rate is worth reading as data. It measures how many bets the organisation has placed without ever deciding which one it wants to win. All projects are swamps, but a team spread across nine of them isn't in a swamp. It's an inch deep in nine of them, being asked why it isn't moving.
