The diagnosis is right and I think it is still generous to the plan.

Look at how your own post is laid out. The Vision, then The Math Made Sense, then What Actually Happened. The first two sections are about the value of the end state. Sixty percent of revenue growing faster, forty percent stable, fifteen percent equity available to partners. All of that is modelling, and I have no reason to doubt any of it.

But none of those numbers were ever in question.

The only genuinely uncertain quantity in the whole exercise was whether several thousand partners would vote to reduce their own position. Everything else in the plan sat downstream of that one answer.

Which means it was not a risk to the project. It was the project.

That is the bit worth naming, because a business case has a habit of doing this. It accumulates confidence about the parts you can model, and it goes quiet about the part you cannot. And the parts you can model are usually the parts nobody was arguing about in the first place. You end up with two hundred pages of certainty stacked on top of a single unpriced assumption, and the stack gets taller while the assumption never moves.

The project is a bet. You size a bet on the thing you are least sure of, not the thing you are most sure of. Do it the other way round and the number you produce is beautifully constructed and describes a world where you have already won.

There is a timing consequence too, and it is the expensive one.

A question like will they vote for this is cheap to ask early and ruinous to ask late. Not because the answer changes, but because spend starts doing the arguing on the plan's behalf. Once a serious number has gone in, the question stops being is this going to pass and becomes are you seriously suggesting we stop now. Those are different meetings and only one of them can be won by evidence.

I do not know when that question was tested inside EY, and neither does anyone reading this from outside it. I only know the general shape, which is that it tends to be asked last, and by then the number you quote, the seven hundred million, is not the cost of finding out. It is the cost of not being allowed to.

Bad news is data, but it is only data if it arrives while it can still change something. Arrive late enough and the same information is just an obituary with a figure attached.

So I would put it slightly differently to you. The partnership model did not kill Project Everest. The partnership model was the ground the whole thing had to be built on, it was visible from the first day, and it was the one part of the plan nobody costed.

Deliver the possible, not the fantasy. The possible version was whatever that partner group would actually have voted for, and it would have been smaller, duller, and open to the charge of timidity from everyone who had read the deck. It also would have happened.

Structure did not stop them seeing the future. It stopped them buying the one they had written down, and that was knowable before a pound was spent.
