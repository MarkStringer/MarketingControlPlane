Decision point rather than red flag is the right correction, and the good and bad split holds up. I would add the thing that sits underneath it, because I think it explains why the split is so hard to apply while a project is running.

The original scope had to compete.

It went into a funding round, or a planning cycle, or a pitch. It was sized, priced, argued for, and it beat other things that wanted the same money. Whatever else you say about that process, it forced a comparison. Somebody had to say this rather than that.

A change request arrives afterwards, and it competes against nothing.

By then the money is committed, the team is standing there, and the only question asked of the new item is whether it is a good idea. On its own, with no rival in the room, almost everything is a good idea. It has a sponsor who wants it, a plausible benefit, and usually a genuinely overlooked condition behind it, exactly as you describe in the good column.

So the test quietly changes from is this better than what we are already doing to is this worth doing. Those sound similar and they are not remotely the same test. The first one has a loser in it. The second one never does.

Which is why I would put the axis somewhere other than good and bad.

The question I want answered is what came out, and who agreed to lose it. Not in the abstract, not as a general acknowledgement that everything has a cost. Name the feature that is now not being built, or the week the date moves, or the contingency that just got spent, and name the person who said yes to that specific loss with their own budget or their own reputation on it.

Do that and it really is a decision point, in the strong sense, because a decision is a thing you can only make by giving something up.

Skip it and what you have is an addition with a rationale attached. It will still be described as good scope creep, and it may well be, but nobody will ever find out, because nothing was tested against it.

There is a second reason to distrust the labels. Good and bad get assigned afterwards. The same change is responsive and commercially astute if the project lands, and it is creep if the project misses, and very often the change had nothing to do with which of those happened. Sorting into two columns feels like judgement and is frequently just knowing the ending.

The project is a bet. A change request is a new bet placed with the stake already on the table, which is the one situation where nobody is required to say what they are taking off the board.

Get the displaced item named in writing and most of your bad column disappears on its own, because the people asking withdraw the request as soon as it costs them something they can see.
