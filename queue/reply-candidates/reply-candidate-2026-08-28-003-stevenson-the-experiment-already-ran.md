Most of the six steps are fine and I would not argue with any of them individually. It is the sentence above them I want to pick at, because I think it is the reason the six steps keep getting run and keep not sticking.

"This isn't an ERP problem. It's a behavior problem."

That puts the fault in the people. Which is comfortable, and I do not think it survives looking at what those people are actually doing.

Someone has been exporting to Excel and rebuilding that report by hand every month for two years. Nobody does that for fun. It is dull, it is fiddly, it happens under time pressure at month end, and there is no credit attached to any of it.

So think about what that person has been doing. They tried the workflow. It did not produce the thing they needed. They built something that did, at their own expense, in their own time, and then they did it again the next month and the month after that, for twenty-four months.

That is not a behaviour problem. That is somebody delivering the close with what they have got.

And it is the best data anybody in that building will ever get about whether the system fits the work. It has been arriving on a monthly schedule for two years, at no cost, and it has been filed as user error.

Here is why it never goes upward, which is the bit I think step four misses.

The four hundred thousand was approved by a person. It was approved on the basis of a specific story about how the work was going to be done afterwards. That story is in a document, and that document has a name on it.

Now try to say, in a room, that the configuration does not match how the work actually happens. In that room it is not a technical observation. It is a comment on somebody's judgement, in front of the people who signed it off with them.

So the workaround stays where it is. Local, undocumented, permanent.

Which reframes your own number rather neatly. The hundred and eighty thousand a year is not what poor adoption costs. It is what an organisation is willing to pay, annually and indefinitely, to avoid reopening a decision. And it is a bargain at the price, because a hundred and eighty thousand of quiet headcount costs nobody their reputation and one honest report might cost somebody theirs.

That is the step that is missing, and no internal champion with authority and accountability can supply it, because they inherit exactly the same incentive as everybody else.

Somebody sufficiently senior has to say, out loud, that the system was configured against a description of the work that turned out not to be true, and that pointing this out is a finding rather than an accusation.

Do that and your six steps will work. Skip it and you will train people who already know what they are doing into a workflow that still does not do it.

You already ran the experiment. It took two years and cost four hundred thousand dollars, and the result is sitting in a spreadsheet somebody built at the weekend.
