Thirty days of listening is good advice and I would take it. But I think the story you tell against yourself is a better argument than the lesson you pull out of it, and it points somewhere slightly different.

Three people had to approve because a VP of Operations got burned on a vendor contract two years earlier and wanted eyes on everything over fifty thousand.

Look at what that control actually is. It is a bet. Placed once, by one person, on the evidence of a single bad night, and it has never been placed again since. Nobody comes back in year two and asks him whether he would still make that trade. The company has no mechanism for that, because nothing in a company ever asks a control to justify itself a second time.

And the cost is paid by people who were not in the room. Every contract that goes through three sets of eyes instead of one is a few days of somebody's week, on deals that have nothing whatever to do with the one that burned him. He carries none of that. He carries only the memory of the thing that went wrong, which is vivid, and gets refreshed every time it does not go wrong again.

So here is the bit I would add to the thirty days, and it is not an argument against listening.

Listening converts this is stupid into this is stupid for a reason. That feels like progress and it usually is. But the second sentence is much harder to act on than the first, and every control in that building has a reason behind it. That is what a control is. A control with no origin story is just a habit. If your test is whether there was a reason, everything passes, and you will leave alone a great deal that should have gone.

The question that pays is not why is this here. You will get a good answer, and it will be true, and it will still be two years out of date.

It is closer to this. If we took it out on Monday, whose name goes on that, and what exactly are they signing. Ask it and you find out something the org chart will never tell you, which is that removing the step means somebody personally underwriting the next bad contract, while keeping it costs them nothing and is not written down anywhere.

That asymmetry is the reason the step is still there. Not the vendor. Not the fifty thousand. Not even the VP, who has probably forgotten.

The project is a bet, and so is every approval gate you inherit. The difference is that a project gets settled eventually, whether anyone likes the answer or not. Most controls never do. They just sit there being paid for.
