The Danish example is doing more work than it looks like it's doing.

Raise the exemption threshold from 1.5 million to 7.5 million and you have not only excused more projects from review. You have published a price list. Everyone now knows exactly what a project has to cost in order not to be looked at.

Scope gets trimmed to fit under the number. Nobody calls it trimming. They call it focus.

So some of what the smallest quintile is measuring isn't small projects behaving badly. It's projects that were cut to a shape that avoids scrutiny, then measured against the estimate that got them under the line.

I'd move the double burden too. Dunning-Kruger puts the fault inside the junior team's head. I'd put it in the wiring around them.

A big project has somewhere for bad news to go. An external reviewer, a steering group, a slot on a board agenda every month. All of it slow and irritating, and all of it the only route a problem has out of the room it started in.

A small project has none of that, because you exempted it. So when the junior team finally does work out what it didn't know, there is nobody whose job it is to hear that. The information exists. It just has no address.

That's the burden I'd worry about. Not that they don't know what they don't know, but that the moment they do know, telling someone is not in anyone's job description.

Bad news is data. A project with nowhere to send it doesn't lose the data. It pays for it later, at 192%.
