The desire path is a good test and the volume heuristic is better than most of what gets offered here. If two people are ignoring it, look at the two people. If ninety are ignoring it, stop looking at the ninety.

I would add a third option that the binary leaves out, because in my experience it is the most common one.

Sometimes the process is not failing. It is working perfectly, at a job nobody wrote down.

Take your laptop example. Ask what that rule produces. It produces a written instruction, a record that the instruction was issued, and a walk round at six o'clock that generates the name of somebody who broke it. Now imagine a laptop actually goes missing. Every one of those artefacts does exactly what it was quietly designed to do, and the organisation is fine, and there is a person to point at.

Compliance was never the deliverable. The deliverable was the rule and the culprit.

Judged against the stated purpose, laptops in drawers, it is a failure and you would expect it to be redesigned. Judged against the actual purpose it is a complete success, and that is why it never gets redesigned no matter how many people ignore it. Nothing is broken. You are looking at the wrong scoreboard.

Here is the test I use, and it is cheap.

Wait until somebody follows the process and the bad thing happens anyway. Then listen to the first sentence out of the room. If it is some version of well, we followed the process, you are looking at a record. If it is some version of then the process is wrong, you are looking at a method.

Only one of those two can be improved by understanding why people are not doing it.

The other thing I would say is about the muddy diagonal itself, because I think it deserves more respect than it gets.

That path is a survey. It is free, it is continuously updated, it has a hundred percent response rate, and it is completely honest because nobody knew they were answering anything. There is no research budget on earth that buys data that good.

And the standard organisational response to it is a fence.

Not a path. A fence, and a sign. Bad news is data, but only when the person receiving it is not the person who has to be wrong for it to count. Laying the tarmac where the mud is means someone's original drawing was mistaken, and that someone usually still works there, usually outranks whoever noticed the mud, and has a perfectly good reason ready about safety or aesthetics or the integrity of the lawn.

So before the people or process question, I would ask a blunter one. Who is this process actually for.

Quite often it is not for the people you are standing there watching at six o'clock. It is for an auditor, an insurer, a regulator or a previous incident, and the people in the corridor are just the raw material it needs to produce its evidence.

Once you know that, the non compliance stops being a puzzle. It is people routing round something that was never built for them, which is what water does, and grass, and everybody who has ever cut a corner across a park.

The path across the grass is the honest survey. The fence is the org chart.
