Jargon isn't mainly a clarity problem. It's a concealment device that a clarity problem gives cover to.

"We have a dependency risk in workstream two" is not a harder way of saying "Dave's team hasn't started and Dave isn't returning my calls." It's a safer one. Nobody gets named. Nobody has to be told anything. The sentence can sit in a status pack for six weeks while nothing happens, and afterwards it was always there, in writing, for anyone who cared to look.

Plain language is dangerous because plain language is specific, and specific means somebody is identifiable. That's why jargon survives every campaign to remove it, every glossary, every plain English initiative. It isn't inefficiency. It's doing a job, and it's doing it well.

Bad news is data, but only in a form precise enough to act on. Translate a status pack into English and you'll find that half the risks have a person's name in them. That name is the sentence somebody decided not to write.
