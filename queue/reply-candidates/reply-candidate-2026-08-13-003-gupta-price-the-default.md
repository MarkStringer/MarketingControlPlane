The five ticks at the top are the best thing in this post and I think they prove something other than what you use them for.

Look at what they establish. The risk was visible. The blocker was written down. The dependency was flagged, the impact was spelled out, and the people with the authority were in the room. Then nothing happened.

Every clarity input was present. So whatever was missing, it was not clarity.

Then the fix is five questions, and four of them are about making the problem more legible. What decision, who decides, what options, what is the impact of delay. Those are fine and I would not argue with any of them individually, but the room you just described had all of that and still went home.

The fifth one is different. What happens if no decision is made.

That is the only question on the list that changes the arithmetic, and I think it is quietly doing all the work.

Because in that room, deciding costs somebody something. Budget, a favour, a reputation, an argument with a peer who outranks them on a good day. Not deciding costs nothing at all. Nobody minutes the meeting where a decision did not get made. There is no artefact, no owner, and no date attached to it, so it never appears anywhere that anyone is held to.

Which means a clearer ask can make things worse rather than better. The crisper you make the question, the more visible a refusal becomes. A vague escalation lets everyone leave without having said no. That is not a failure of your framing. That is the room preferring the version it can survive, and it will keep preferring it however well you write the slide.

So the thing that moves it is not a better question. It is removing the free option.

Not "we need a decision on A or B", because that can be received, thanked, and shelved. Something closer to: unless somebody says otherwise by Friday, we are doing A, this is what A costs, and this is what it makes harder later.

Now silence is a decision with a name and a date on it. In my experience you often get an answer inside a day, and quite often it is not A, which tells you the decision was available the whole time and only the mechanism was missing.

Here is why PMs do not do this, and it is not laziness.

A dated default puts the person who wrote it on the hook. If Friday passes, you do A, and the milestone does not move after all, then the consequence you priced was not real, and you were wrong in front of exactly the audience you were trying to impress. Asking a clear question is safe. Naming a default is a bet.

That is the bit worth taking on. The project is a bet anyway, whether or not anyone writes the odds down, and the only real question is whether the bet is stated where it can be checked or buried where it cannot.

Bad news is data, but only when it is dated. An undated risk is a conversation and everybody has been to that conversation. A dated one is a decision waiting to happen, and it happens whether or not anyone turns up.
