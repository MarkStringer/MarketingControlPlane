Nobody reporting to you is the accurate part, and it is worth sitting with rather than solving.

But look at what the advice says you need. Know the market. Know the competition. Be clear with engineering. Integrate all of it.

Every one of those is about being right.

Being right is not the currency in a room where you cannot fire anyone. I have watched someone be comprehensively right about a market and lose the argument anyway, to a person who was vaguer but had been saying the same vague thing for two years. What travels without authority is not correctness. It is whether people think you will still be here, and still saying this, when the bill arrives.

Which is why the last line does more damage than it looks. What matters is that the product wins.

It does matter. But a product winning or losing shows up a year and a half after the decisions that caused it, and by then the room has quietly rewritten who said what. If it wins, everyone who agreed with you was aligned from the start. If it loses, you were the one who pushed for it. That asymmetry does not fall evenly on the person with no formal power.

The project is a bet. So is the product. So the useful move is not becoming more persuasive, it is writing the bet down while it is still a bet. What you think will happen, by when, and what you will accept as evidence you were wrong.

That is a much less impressive skill than influence. It also works when people have gone off you, which influence does not.

One more thing, and it is not a cheap shot. This advice comes from someone who could fire people. The hard part of having no authority was never working out what to do. It is that the person explaining it to you had a way out of the room that you do not have.
