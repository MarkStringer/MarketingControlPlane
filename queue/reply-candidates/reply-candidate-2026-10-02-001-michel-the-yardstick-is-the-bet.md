The yardstick point is right, and it cuts both ways.

If the measure is picked after the results are in, a failed project can always be rescued by redefining success as "learning". The 3 to 10 year payback is the same trick with a longer fuse.

The project is a bet. A bet has a stake, a payoff and a date you'll find out. Write those down before the money goes in and "learning" is a legitimate return, because you said in advance what you expected to learn and by when.

Without that, you aren't measuring ROI. You're choosing the yardstick that flatters whatever happened.
