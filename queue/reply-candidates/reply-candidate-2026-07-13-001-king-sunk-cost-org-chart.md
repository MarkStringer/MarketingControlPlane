post_summary: Callum King argues that large projects frequently fail because decision-makers keep investing based on money already spent rather than an honest assessment of future value. He frames this as the sunk cost fallacy, cites Bent Flyvbjerg's "How Big Things Get Done" (of 16,000 projects only 8.5 percent hit both cost and time, and 0.5 percent hit time, cost and benefit), and uses Concorde as an example of a project driven on by prior investment despite evidence it was not viable. His recommendation is that project managers should decide on future value and alignment with evolving strategic goals rather than on past spend.
---

The fallacy is real, but the label is a little too kind. It makes carrying on sound like a thinking error, the kind of thing you could fix by showing people the Flyvbjerg numbers.

You can show them. They'll nod. They'll fund it again next quarter.

Because the sunk cost that actually decides these things isn't in the accounts. It's in the org chart. The money already spent belongs to the company. The reputation staked on the business case belongs to a specific person, and that person is usually the one who would have to stand up and call it. For them, cancelling isn't a rational calculation about future value. It's a public confession.

The project is a bet, and nobody folds when the chips on the table have their own name written on them. Concorde didn't keep going because the maths kept working. It kept going because too many careers had already taken off with it.

So if you want fewer zombie projects, the Flyvbjerg data isn't the lever. Make it cheaper to be the person who says stop. Bad news is data, but only in a room where the messenger still has a job the morning after.
