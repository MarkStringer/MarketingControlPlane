Politicians get a mandate. They also get to lose.

That is the part the comparison misses. A politician can say no in public and survive it, because somebody voted for them, and that somebody is a source of power independent of the people being told no. Take that away and you are not doing politics. You are absorbing it.

Which is what your list actually describes, and I do not think it means to. Read it back. Every item on it is a responsibility. Not one of them is a power. Be diplomatic. Hold people accountable. Push without being pushy. Keep everyone calm. That is the job description of a shock absorber, and shock absorbers are consumables.

But the line I want to stop on is this one. Deliver bad news in a way that somehow still sounds optimistic.

I know exactly why it is on the list, because I did it for years and I was good at it. Here is what I eventually worked out. If bad news has to turn up dressed as optimism before anyone will accept delivery, then the organisation has quietly stopped taking data. I was not being diplomatic. I was being a filter.

Bad news is data. Most of the value of a project manager is being the person who finds out early that the thing is worse than the plan says. If the same job also requires you to make that discovery sound survivable to the people who wrote the plan, you have been handed two tasks that cancel each other out, and only one of them gets you invited back.

So I would take one thing off your ending. It is not that the toughest project is the people. People are mostly fine. The toughest project is being answerable for an outcome you have no authority to cause, and then being told that what you need for that is patience.

It is not patience. It is a sponsor who can hear a no without going looking for somebody to attach it to. Find one of those and about half the items on your list stop being necessary.
