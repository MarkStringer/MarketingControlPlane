You are right about the ambush, and the courtroom drama version of a review is genuinely a waste of everyone's afternoon. I have sat in those. Nobody's mind was changed, and the loudest person won.

But the shadow campaign costs you something you are not counting.

An objection you handle one to one stays one to one. You have converted a shared fact into a set of private ones. Everybody now knows the risk, and nobody in that room has heard anybody else say it.

Eight months later, when it goes the way the quiet person said it would, every single one of them can say in good conscience that they thought it was fine. And they will be telling the truth, because the only place the doubt was ever spoken out loud was a corridor.

The reason to say the uncomfortable thing in the room, with everyone sitting there, is not drama. It is that afterwards there is a date on it. The organisation knew, on the fifteenth, in front of nine people.

There is a second problem, and it is the one I would actually worry about. A boring meeting means the disagreement got resolved. A boring meeting also means the disagreement went underground and everyone learned that the room is not where you say things. From inside the room those look identical.

You can tell the difference because you did the individual work and you know what is in everyone's head. The person who reads your post and just starts aiming for boring cannot tell the difference at all. They get the second one and score it as the first, because absence of drama is satisfied by both.

Boring is fine. Boring and unwitnessed is how a project ends up with a failure that everybody privately expected and nobody can say when.
