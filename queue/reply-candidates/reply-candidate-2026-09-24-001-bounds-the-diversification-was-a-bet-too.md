Nobody disputes the fix. Fewer pieces, faster cycles, sold-off theme parks, closer to the fans. It worked, and it worked fast.

But the more interesting question is why it took getting to the edge of bankruptcy to see it.

Every one of those things Knudstorp cut had, at some point, looked like a reasonable extension of a business that was already working. Theme parks. Clothing lines. Video games. Exotic parts nobody had asked for. Each one got approved the way a strong core business gets to approve things: as an extension, not as a wager.

That's the trick. A bet doesn't stop being a bet because it's attached to a company that's currently winning. It just stops looking like one.

Nobody was tracking the combined exposure of a dozen "obviously fine" extensions, because no single extension was ever reviewed as a bet in its own right. The turnaround wasn't really about focus. It was about someone finally sitting down and adding up what had quietly been staked, all at once, for the first time.

Most companies don't get a Knudstorp. They get to keep not adding it up until the number does it for them.
