The 80 against the 95 is the whole post and I would not argue with the diagnosis. Cost against budget tells you what you have spent. It tells you nothing about what is standing on site.

I would also grant the small thing, which is that the Super's 80% was not really a measurement. It was an answer to a different question, which was how much trouble am I in, and he gave the number that bought him another two weeks. You will get that number from anyone who has not been given a better one to give.

But I think the last paragraph quietly undoes the rest.

You want the daily rate to be an early warning mechanism. Then you say the target should be set on what good looks like rather than on prior performance, because historical numbers estimate to your own inefficiencies.

Both of those are reasonable. They are not reasonable at the same time, on the same number.

Here is the thing about an alarm. It only works if going off means something. If the rate is set at what good looks like, and this crew on this job has never once hit what good looks like, then behind is the normal condition of the job. Week one behind. Week two behind. Week three behind, and the Super has by now got a decent explanation ready, because he has needed one three times.

By week four nobody in that meeting is reading the number. They have learned, correctly, that it runs red on a healthy job.

And then something actually breaks. A supplier slips, the sequence goes wrong, you lose two people to another site. On the report it looks exactly the same as the previous three weeks. That is the failure mode, and it is worse than having no rate at all, because now everybody believes there is an early warning system.

So I would split it rather than argue with either half. One number is what this crew can do here, on this job, with this sequence, taken from what they have actually done. That is the warning line, and crossing it has to be surprising or it is not a line. The other number is what good looks like, and that is an estimating argument and a training argument, and it is worth having, just nowhere near the WIP meeting.

Put both jobs on one number and the number becomes explainable, and an explainable number is not an instrument.

Bad news is data, but only when the thing reporting it is trusted. Set the alarm to an aspiration and you have not built early warning. You have built a second thing for the field to explain away, on top of the percent complete they were already guessing at.
