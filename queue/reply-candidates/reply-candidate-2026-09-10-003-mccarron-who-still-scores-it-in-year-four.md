The two questions are the real insight here. Everything else is presentation.

What's worth naming is what happens to a scorecard once leadership has seen it a few times. Year
one, it's diagnostic. Nobody's job depends on the score, so nobody has a reason to shade it, and it
does exactly what you describe: makes the invisible visible.

By year three, the score is attached to budget asks and performance conversations. That's when the
incentive flips. Moving from "foundational" to "intermediate" starts to matter more than whether
the underlying throughput actually improved, and the grid that once forced honesty starts rewarding
whoever describes their own function most favourably.

Bad news is data, but only while nothing rides on it staying bad. The scorecard that built your
case for investment in year one is the same scorecard someone will eventually be tempted to grade
generously in year four. Worth deciding now who scores it, and whether that person still has
anything to gain from the number moving.
