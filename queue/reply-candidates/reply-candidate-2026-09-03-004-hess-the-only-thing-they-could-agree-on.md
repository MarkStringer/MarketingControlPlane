All three of these are real, and the first one is the one that actually kills people. The other two you can usually survive. That one you cannot.

But I would read your own example differently to the way you read it.

CEO wants real-time reporting. CFO wants cost control. COO wants operational efficiency. Month five, they fire you. You say they fired you because they could not decide what to deliver.

I think the firing was the decision.

It was the only one on the table where none of those three lost anything to the other two. Every other route out of that room required one of them to concede in front of the others, and none of them was going to do that over an implementation. You were the one option they could all say yes to, and they could say yes to it quickly, and afterwards nobody had to explain themselves to anybody.

That is what an outside firm is in a room like that, on a job like that, and it has nothing whatever to do with SAP.

Which is where I think your filter runs into trouble, and this is meant as a practical problem rather than a criticism.

To refuse a job on reason number one, you need those three people to disagree in front of you. Pre-sales is the exact phase in which they will not. The scope was written loosely enough to fund, which means it was written loosely enough to mean three different things, and it stayed loose because tightening it would have stopped the money before it started. Nobody was hiding anything from you that they were not also hiding from each other.

So the disagreement does not turn up looking like disagreement. It turns up as unusually smooth agreement about an unusually vague objective, from a group who normally argue about the coffee. That is the tell, and it reads as a good sign right up until month three.

Which means can you agree on what success looks like is the wrong question at the door, because they will say yes and they will mean it. Everybody can agree on real-time reporting and cost control and operational efficiency in the abstract. It is only in the build that they turn out to be three different systems.

The question I would put instead is which of you is worse off if this lands exactly as written.

If nobody can answer that, the definition is not finished, whatever the document says. If all three can answer it and are still in the room, you have got a client. Deliver the possible, not the fantasy, and the fantasy on that job was never the timeline. It was that one system was going to settle an argument three executives had already agreed not to have.

Saying no to that is worth more than the five hundred thousand. You are just never going to get to say it at the door.
