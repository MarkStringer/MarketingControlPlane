Your daughter has a stronger case than the one you answered.

She said you don't do any real work. You replied that you coordinate the work. That is a concession rather than a rebuttal, and it is the concession that has been quietly eating the role for twenty years. Coordination is exactly the part that gets automated, outsourced, absorbed into a tool, or handed to whoever on the team is most organised. If aligning people, tasks and timelines is the case for the defence, the defence loses slowly.

Here is the part she cannot see yet, because you only get to see it from inside.

Every project is a bet. Somebody has decided to spend real money on an outcome that is not certain, and the odds move every week. Most of the organisation has an incentive not to look at the odds. The engineers have their own bit to worry about, the sponsor has a number they said out loud in a board meeting, and everyone downstream has learned that putting a hand up is expensive.

The job nobody else will do is being the person who says the true thing about the odds, out loud, on a Tuesday, when it is unwelcome.

That is not glamorous either, and it is considerably harder than the meetings. Bad news is data, and the project manager is usually the only person who touches all of it and is allowed in the room where it lands. When that person stops carrying it, the project does not fall over straight away. It stays green for another four months and then falls over all at once.

So I would answer her differently. Not "without me nothing gets built", because plenty gets built without us. More like: without somebody whose actual job is to know what is really going on, things get built for a long time after everyone involved has quietly stopped believing in them.

The meetings are the visible part. Being willing to be the unpopular voice in them is the job.
