The practical advice is sound and I would take it. It is the framing I want to push on, because I think it lets the rest of us off far too lightly.

Ninety-two out of a hundred sounds like a Japanese characteristic. Sit in a European steering committee for an hour and tell me you are watching a low avoidance culture at work.

Change control boards. Stage gates. RAG reports that get softened on the way up. Assurance reviews of the assurance function. Somebody paid to write a risk register that nobody has opened since the second month.

We are every bit as frightened. We have simply arranged to be frightened later.

And that is the difference worth naming, because it is a difference in cost rather than in temperament. Questions asked before anything exists are expensive in weeks and almost free in money. Nothing has been built, nobody has announced anything, and changing your mind still costs nothing but a meeting.

The same questions asked eighteen months in are cheap in weeks and ruinous in money, because by then the concrete has set, the date is public, and every remaining option is one of the bad ones.

Move fast and break things is not the absence of uncertainty avoidance. It is uncertainty avoidance on credit, and the interest is paid by whoever is holding the project when the questions finally arrive.

So when a counterpart asks for the test data and the contingency plan, "a roadmap to what they need to say yes" is generous but it undersells them. They are asking at the point where the answer can still change what gets built. That is the only point at which the answer is worth the paper.

Everybody eventually asks those questions. The Japanese side is just asking while the answers are still cheap.
