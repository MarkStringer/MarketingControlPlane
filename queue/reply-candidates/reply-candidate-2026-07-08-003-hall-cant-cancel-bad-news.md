His argument is that surviving a crisis is about leading smarter, not working harder, via five practices, humility over ego ("when assumptions fail, ego must go, don't double down on a bad plan"), intentional alignment, standards as survival, rapid course correction, and change-management discipline. He notes that in defense work you cannot cancel or shelve a project, so you must press forward with structural integrity.
---

Strong list, and the defense angle is the interesting part, because you name the one case where the usual escape hatch is bolted shut. You can't cancel it, can't shelve it, can't quietly rescope it out of existence.

When you can't kill a project, the only lever left is how early the truth reaches someone who can still do something with it. Standards and real-time documentation matter for exactly that reason. Not as overhead, but as the plumbing that carries bad news while it's still small. Bad news is data, and on a project you're not allowed to abandon it's the only data that helps.

Humility over ego is the same point wearing a nicer suit. Dropping a bad plan means someone has to say out loud that the bet went the wrong way, and no amount of alignment makes that safe if saying it gets you punished.
