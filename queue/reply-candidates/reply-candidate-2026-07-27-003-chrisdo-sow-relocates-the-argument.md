post_summary: Chris Do argues that scope creep on client work is prevented by a clear Statement of Work. He frames the core problem as "vague agreements, fuzzy boundaries, good intentions" leading to "six weeks later you're bleeding money and everyone's frustrated". His prescribed SOW components are exact deliverables (his example is "3 homepage concepts, 2 rounds of revisions"), specified hours of operation, defined revision rounds with change limits, and feedback cycle timelines with stated consequences. He also recommends collecting all client feedback before acting on any of it, resolving contradictions with the client first, then presenting one unified set of changes, rather than responding to each piece of feedback as it arrives. His closing claim is "Scope creep isn't a them problem. It's a you problem. And that's good news, because that means you are in control." The clause worth keeping is the numbers. Three concepts, two rounds. That is not paperwork, that is you writing down a bet and putting a price on it.

Here is what it does not do though. It does not stop the client discovering what they want. At the point of signing your client does not actually know. They know it in a rough, gestural, mostly wrong way, and the thing that teaches them is looking at your work. That is not a character defect and no amount of pre-agreement removes it, because the information does not exist yet at the moment you are both signing.

So the SOW does not end the argument. It relocates it. Instead of arguing about whether the extra work is reasonable, you now argue about whether this counts as a round. I have sat in that meeting. It is a much better meeting than the one you get with no contract at all, but it is not a peaceful one, and anyone sold the SOW as the end of scope creep is in for a surprise around week five.

I would be careful with the last line too. It is a you problem, so you are in control. Half of that is true and useful. The other half quietly makes you accountable for something you were never in a position to forecast, and that is how a person ends up absorbing round three for free to prove they are a professional.

The bet you are really placing is that what the client understands in week zero survives them seeing the thing. Sometimes it does. Price the rounds so that when it does not, the conversation is about money rather than about whose fault it is.
