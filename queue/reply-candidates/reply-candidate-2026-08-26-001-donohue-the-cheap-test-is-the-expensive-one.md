The split is real. But the £500k is not the interesting number in that sentence. The £0 is.

Nobody sits in a room and decides to skip the desirability test. What happens is that it never gets commissioned, and there is a sound reason for that which has nothing to do with anyone misunderstanding risk.

Here's the thing. The two tests have completely different consequences for the person who orders them.

A feasibility study has a safe failure mode. If it comes back hard, you have not discovered that you were wrong, you have discovered a technical challenge. Technical challenges attract budget, headcount and sympathy. Everyone involved looks diligent and the work carries on.

A desirability test has no safe failure mode. It is quick, it costs almost nothing, and if the answer is no then somebody has to account for why the thing was funded in the first place. There is no version of that result which leaves the sponsor better off than not knowing.

So the expensive test gets bought because it is cheap to survive, and the cheap test gets skipped because it is expensive to survive. That is not a budgeting error. It is people responding sensibly to what happens to them afterwards.

Which is why mapping the assumptions will not move the money on its own. The assumptions were never the hard part. Most people in the building could tell you on the way to the car park which one is load bearing and which one nobody has checked.

You call it a product bet, and that is the right word. The catch with a bet is that somebody has to be willing to lose it. Until the person holding the budget can say "it turned out nobody wanted it" out loud and still have a job on Monday, the £0 stays at £0 and the £500k keeps buying the only answer that is safe to receive.
