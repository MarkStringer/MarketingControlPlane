Agreed on the economics, and the economics is the least interesting part of this.

Nobody I have watched continue a doomed project was confused about sunk costs. They could all explain the fallacy. One of them had a slide about it.

Here is the thing. Your test is whether you would walk away if nothing had been invested. Inside an organisation that question is not answerable, because the money did not simply vanish. It bought two things.

The first is knowledge. Eighteen months of spend is eighteen months of finding out what is genuinely hard, who is quietly objecting, which integration turns out to be a swamp. You cannot pretend not to know that. So the honest version of your question is not "would I start this from zero", it is "would I start this today knowing what the last eighteen months taught me". That is a much better question, and the answer is usually a different project. Smaller. With the fantasy trimmed off.

The second thing the money bought is a public position. Somebody stood up and said this was worth doing. Stopping does not just reallocate capital, it publishes a verdict on that person. The spend is irrelevant to the organisation's decision and entirely relevant to the decider's, and those are two different decisions even though only one of them ever appears on the slide.

Which is why explaining the fallacy so rarely changes anything. They are not making an error. They are being accurate about their own exposure, and the organisation taught them what that exposure is.

So if you want sunk cost thinking to stop, do not run a session on cognitive biases. Find the last three things your organisation cancelled and look at what happened to the people who cancelled them. That is the number your executives are actually running, and it is the only one they can be certain of.

Bad news is data, right until the moment when delivering it costs the deliverer more than sitting on it does. Then it becomes a liability, and everybody learns to hold on to it a bit longer.
