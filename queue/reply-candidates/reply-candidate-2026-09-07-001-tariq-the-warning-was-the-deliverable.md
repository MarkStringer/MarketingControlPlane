An Early Warning clause looks like admin because the thing it asks for feels redundant. You already know there is contaminated ground. You are already fixing it. Why file a notice about a problem you are already solving.

Because the notice was never about the problem. It was about who gets to see the problem while it is still cheap to do something about it.

The contractor in this story did the actual work well. The soil came out, the regulator got told, the site kept moving. What did not happen is the client got a chance to route around the expensive path before it was taken. That is a different deliverable to "did you fix the ground," and the contract prices it separately.

Bad news is data, but data has an expiry date. On day one the contaminated ground is worth £900K and four weeks. By week six it is worth £1.5M and a relationship that does not survive adjudication. The information never changed. The window to act on it did, and that window is the thing the clause is actually protecting.

The contractor's defence was that the outcome would have been the same either way. Nobody can know that, including the contractor, because the client never got the chance to try. That is the real cost of staying quiet. Not that it hides the problem. That it hides the version of the story where someone else got to make it smaller.
