I have never met anyone who set out to design bad governance.

That is the bit that makes this hard. Every stage gate in the world started life as a guardrail. Somebody sat down, drew the decision steps, wrote the templates, agreed the criteria, and it all worked rather well for about eighteen months.

So if nobody designs the bad version, something else is turning the good version into it.

I sat on a gate review once where the criteria were clear, the pack was complete, and the honest answer was no. We said yes. Not because anyone was corrupt or stupid, but because the person who wanted the yes was in the room, and the next gate was six weeks away and might be somebody else's problem by then.

Nothing about the design changed that afternoon. What changed was that the governance was finally asked to produce a no, and nobody would carry it.

That is the moment. Good governance becomes bad governance the first time it has to hand a senior person an answer they do not want, and no clarity of criteria prevents that, because the criteria were never what was doing the work.

Here is the thing. Governance speed is not a property of the framework. It is a measure of how much bad news your organisation can absorb without charging someone for delivering it. Where a red status costs you your reputation, your gates will be slow, and they will be slow in a very specific way: all the real deciding happens in corridors and pre-meetings, and the gate itself becomes a ceremony for announcing a decision that was made somewhere with no minutes. Where bad news is data, the gate is quick, because there is nothing to manage on the way in.

The templates are fine. Keep the templates.

Then go and check the thing underneath them. Ask when your governance last said no to something the sponsor wanted, and find out what happened to the person who said it. If nobody can remember an example, you do not have guardrails. You have a corridor.
