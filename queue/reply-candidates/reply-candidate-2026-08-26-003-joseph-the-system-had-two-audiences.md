The 80 SOPs and the hundreds of forms is the part of this worth staring at, and I do not think it was a sizing error.

Somebody chose that system. It was not forced on them. And for the job it was actually bought to do, it worked.

It got through a due diligence conversation. It gave an auditor, a notified body and an investor a complete answer without anybody at the company having to describe how the work really gets done. As a document for those readers it was excellent, and it would have been worse if it had been smaller.

The trouble is that it had two audiences and only one of them worked there.

The people outside the building got a system that reads well. The people inside got one that cost more to comply with than to route around, so they routed around it. That was entirely predictable and it is also the only honest measurement anyone took of the thing all year.

Which gives you a test that survives the shrinking. Before adding any procedure, incomplete or otherwise, ask who this is written for. If the answer is somebody who will read it once and never do the work it describes, you have not built a quality system. You have built an exhibit, and exhibits do not catch anything.

That test matters because thin documents can be written for the wrong reader just as easily as thick ones. Slimmed down draft SOPs are genuinely better, but not because they are shorter. They are better because they are harder to write for an audience that is never going to follow them.

A thin procedure people actually use tells you what is true about your process. A thick one they work around tells you what somebody wished was true. The second sort costs more, and it is the sort that gets bought.

Those employees ignoring the system generated the most useful piece of information that startup produced all year. The expensive part is that nobody wrote it down.
