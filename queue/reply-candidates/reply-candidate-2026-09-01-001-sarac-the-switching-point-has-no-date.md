You are right that everything in that model is a forecast, and I think the sensitivity analysis is the most useful thing in the whole approval pack. It is also the part that gets used least.

Look at what it actually produces. Not a description of risk, but a number. Ramp-up at fourteen months instead of nine and the recommendation flips. That is a decision rule, written down in advance, by people who were calm and had time and no skin in that particular week.

Then the Board approves, and the switching point goes in the appendix.

Month ten arrives. Somebody on site knows perfectly well that ramp-up is going to run to fourteen. That is not a variance. It is the exact condition the Board was shown and told the Board it would not accept. But there is no meeting for it, no owner and no trigger, so it turns up as a line in a monthly report, collects a sentence about commissioning learnings, and the project carries on.

Bad news is data, but only when it has somewhere to be delivered. The sensitivity analysis writes the address and then nobody puts it on the envelope.

On optimism I would put it slightly differently to you. It is not really a failure of good faith. At the approval gate only one side of the range carries any consequence. Nobody has ever had a project stopped for being too optimistic about ramp-up, because the number is not tested when it is set, it is tested years later by different people in different jobs. Optimism there is not a bias. It is an accurate reading of how the process scores you.

Which is why I would spend rather less effort on getting the assumption right and rather more on making the switching point a scheduled re-decision. Give it a date, give it a name, and put it in the plan next to the milestones.

Approximately right is fine. Approximately right and never revisited is just precisely wrong with a longer fuse.
