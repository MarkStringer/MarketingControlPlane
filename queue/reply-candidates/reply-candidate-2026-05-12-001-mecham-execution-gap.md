"Poor execution" is doing a lot of work here.

When 67% of plans fail at the execution stage, that number is telling you something about the plans, not the people executing them.

From inside those projects, it rarely looks like execution failure. It looks like the plan was wrong about what the work would take. Wrong about the dependencies. Wrong about what the client actually wanted once they saw it.

That's not strategy failing because of execution. That's the bet coming back with better information than you had when you made it.

If 67% of plans consistently fail in execution, the honest conclusion is that 67% of plans were wrong when they were written — not badly managed afterward. Bad news from the execution stage isn't a failure of discipline. It's data.

The phrase "robust execution framework" doesn't fix that. It just creates a more elaborate structure for documenting why the plan was wrong.
