The pattern you describe at the front is exactly right, and it is worth saying that most people arguing about deadlines never get that far. Weeks of nothing, then a fortnight of heroics, then something that shipped on the day and was fine. I have watched that happen more times than I would like to write down.

I think two things got deleted here, though, and the wrong one is getting the credit.

You did not just remove dates. You removed the cost of giving an honest one.

Consider what the question "when will this actually be ready to be great?" sounds like depending on who is asking it. Coming from you, it is a real question, because you are the person who can absorb the answer. Nothing bad happens to the person who says six weeks.

Now put that same question in the mouth of a delivery manager three layers down. The honest answer goes to somebody who has already told somebody else a different number. It does not arrive as information. It arrives as an opening bid in a negotiation nobody has admitted is happening, and it gets treated accordingly.

So the thing you did that actually worked, and the thing almost nobody reading this can copy, is that you made honesty about time free. Everyone else is being asked for an honest date by a person who cannot afford one.

The other bit I would push on is what the dates were ever for.

Both your examples are a single team finishing a single thing with nobody standing behind them waiting. In that situation a deadline really is doing very little except supplying a date to panic about, and taking it away costs you nothing.

But deadlines are not primarily a motivational device for the people doing the work. They are a synchronisation device for everybody who is not. The date exists so that the people who cannot see your work can plan around it.

Which gives you a rather good test. Remove the date, and if nothing anywhere else in the organisation notices, you have just proved that nothing was ever waiting on it. Useful discovery. Quite a lot of corporate deadlines would fail that test and deserve to.

But the moment three teams have to arrive at the same place, someone has to stand up and name a date that is going to be wrong. Not because the date is true. Because being wrong early, in public, where other people can adjust, is worth considerably more than being honest late and alone.

A date you owe nobody is not a deadline. It is a preference, and you should absolutely be free to change it.
