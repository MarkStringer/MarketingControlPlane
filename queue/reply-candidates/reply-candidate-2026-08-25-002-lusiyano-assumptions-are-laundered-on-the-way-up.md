Agreed on the diagnosis, and the tool list is the right tool list. Scenario analysis, sensitivity, driver-based modelling, a proper pre-mortem. None of that is controversial and most boards can name all six.

Which is the problem with it.

Here's the thing. The boards I have watched get badly surprised were not short of technique. They had the risk committee. They had the severe case in the appendix. They got surprised anyway, and I do not think it was because nobody asked your question.

A plan arriving in a board pack is not an analysis. It is a request. Somebody wants the money, and every assumption in that document is load-bearing for the ask. Asking what must be true, in that room, on that morning, is asking the person who wrote it to argue against their own paper in front of the people who decide what happens to them next year. They will do it politely and they will do it thinly.

But the real editing happened long before the meeting.

That paper came up through four or five hands. At every hand, somebody looked at the caveat covering their own section and made it a little smaller, because uncertainty travelling upward does not read as rigour. It reads as not being on top of your job. Nobody lied. Everybody rounded.

So the assumptions in front of your directors are not unexamined. They are laundered. The bits that could have failed a stress test were taken out at grade six, and what arrives at board level is a document that has been pre-cleared of the things you want to interrogate. You can run every scenario on the list against it and get a confident answer, because the numbers you are testing were selected for their ability to survive being tested.

Bad news is data, right up until carrying it upstairs costs the carrier more than sitting on it does. Then it stops being data and becomes a career decision, and it gets made the same way every time.

Which means the honest version of your last line is slightly different. Strong boards do not test assumptions before reality does. Strong boards find out whether it is survivable to bring them a bad one.

If you want to know what your scenario analysis is actually worth, do not audit the model. Find the last three people who put a genuine severe case in a board paper and look at what happened to them afterwards. Your executives already know that answer. It is the only stress test any of them have run.
