I think you have correctly identified the gap and then misfiled it as a software problem.

Both of the things you say P6 is bad at have something in common, and it is not interactivity. Top down planning and site planning are the two places where the plan stops being a calculation and becomes an argument between people with different exposures.

Top down, somebody has already said a date out loud. Possibly to a board, possibly to a regulator, possibly to a market. The top down plan is not being derived, it is being reconciled backwards to a number that already exists and is now politically expensive to move. The work is finding out who can survive being wrong, and by how much.

Site planning is the same problem at the other end of the telescope. The two week look ahead is a negotiation between a foreman who knows what is actually going to happen and a schedule that says something else, conducted in front of people who will have to explain the variance.

No tool ships that. What a tool can do is decide how visible the argument is, and P6 makes it very hard to see, which is not always considered a defect by everyone who buys it. A schedule that takes a specialist a fortnight to interrogate is a schedule nobody senior interrogates. The steep learning curve you list as a weakness is doing quiet work for somebody.

So I would be careful what you wish for from the breakthrough. A genuinely interactive planning tool would surface disagreement between stakeholders early, at speed, with names attached. That is exactly the product nobody who has to present the number wants sitting in the room.

The reason the last twenty years of tooling has not delivered it is not that Oracle could not build it.
