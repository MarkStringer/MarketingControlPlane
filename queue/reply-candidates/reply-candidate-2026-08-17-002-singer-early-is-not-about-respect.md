People are usually upset about the silence, not the slip. I think that is true, and I think it is true of a narrower band of slips than it sounds.

Look at your first piece of advice, though. Set deadlines with buffer.

If this were really a communication problem you would not need the buffer. You put it there because you already know the date is a guess and you would rather not have that particular conversation out loud. It is a small untruth told early to avoid a larger one later. It is usually the right call. It just is not communication.

Here is the thing about telling people early. I think you have got the reason slightly wrong, and the reason turns out to matter.

Early updates do not work because they show respect and maturity. They work because they hand the decision back while the decision still exists. Tell someone on day three that you are going to be two weeks late and they can move something, cut something, or go and warn somebody. Tell them on day forty and you have not communicated at all. You have delivered a fact and left them to absorb it. Same information, same tone, entirely different object.

That is not a pedantic distinction, because respect degrades and options do not. Communicate early and often, held as a matter of courtesy, turns into a weekly update. Weekly updates fill up with activity. I have sat on projects carrying an enormous volume of communication with almost no bad news anywhere in it. Everybody was diligent. Everybody was polite. Nothing anyone sent gave anybody else a choice.

Bad news is data. But only the kind that arrives while someone can still act on it.

So the test I would put on an update is not whether it went out on time, or whether it was gracious about the slip. It is whether the person receiving it can now do something they could not have done the day before. If they cannot, you have not communicated. You have complied.
