The self-criticism is the good part and it is rarer than it should be. Most posts about accountability point downward. Yours points at you, and "I called it being understanding, it was really avoidance" is an uncomfortable sentence to publish.

So I want to take the argument seriously rather than agree with it.

Test 2 and Test 3 are pulling against each other, and I do not think the post notices.

Test 2 raises the cost of falling short in front of your peers. That is the mechanism, you say so directly, nobody wants to be the one who missed in public. Fine. But that price applies to every version of falling short, including the useful one.

Here's the thing. In week two, somebody on that team can already tell the date is wrong. They nearly always can. Saying so in week two is cheap for the project and expensive for them, because in a room where commitments are visible, being the first to say "this will not land" is indistinguishable from being the first to fail. So they do not say it in week two. They say it in week nine, when the calendar says it for them and nobody can be singled out.

You have got more accountability and less forecasting. The misses stop being early and start being late, and a late miss is the only kind you cannot do anything about.

Which brings me to what a slipped deadline actually is.

It is a measurement. Probably the only unbiased one you will get all quarter. The team has just told you, at some cost to themselves, what that work really takes, and unlike every estimate that preceded it, this number was not produced by anyone hoping to please you.

Test 3 spends it. Diagnose and improve sounds neutral, but it puts the person under the light rather than the date, and it starts from the assumption that the date was sound and the delivery was not. Sometimes true. Often the date was somebody else's answer in somebody else's meeting, and the team have been quietly paying for it ever since.

Notice that your Clarity Test cannot catch that. It asks whether they know what is due and when. It never asks where the when came from, or whether a single person in the room believed it when they heard it. A completely fictional date passes the Clarity Test the moment everyone can recite it.

Bad news is data. But data only arrives if the person holding it is not charged for the delivery, and public commitment is a charge.

So I would keep your honesty and change one thing. You did not let that deadline slide. The deadline was wrong, and the slide was how you found out.
