You are right, and I want to add the reason it keeps happening, because I do not think it is mostly meanness.

Look again at your own comparison. Venues, consultants, printing, catering. Every one of those turns up with an invoice attached and somebody outside the organisation chasing payment. They go into the budget at the very start, because leaving them out creates an immediate and personal problem for whoever is writing the budget.

Involvement sends no invoice. Nobody chases it. So it never enters the plan, and that is the part that does the damage, because work that is not in the plan cannot be late, cannot be under-delivered, and cannot be reported on.

Unpaid work is unfalsifiable. You can run a project that promised meaningful public involvement, do very little of it, and finish with a completely clean record, because there was never a number there to miss.

That is a far more comfortable arrangement than it looks from the outside, and it is why it keeps surviving perfectly sincere good intentions. Nobody has to defend it. Nobody is even asked.

Putting a real figure against involvement changes the fairness, which is your argument and it stands on its own. What it also changes is that the work becomes checkable. It acquires a cost, a scope, and somebody who has to say what was delivered for the money.

It means it can also be cut. I know that sounds like an argument against and it is the exact opposite. A line that can be cut is a line that has to be argued for in the room where the money is actually decided. Free work never gets into that room. It gets thanked in the introduction and left out of the plan.

Expertise that costs nothing is not being valued. It is being kept off the books.
