Between whom. That is the right question and almost nobody asks it, because "we need better communication" is one of those sentences that sounds like a diagnosis and is actually a way of ending the conversation.

The mechanism you name is real too. Unclear ownership, so everyone gets copied, so the meeting runs long, so somebody schedules a follow up.

Where I would push is on why the ownership is unclear.

In your post it reads like an oversight. Something nobody got round to writing down, fixable with a better document. Sometimes that is exactly what it is, usually in young companies that grew faster than their paperwork.

More often, in my experience, the vagueness was the deal.

Two directors wanted different things. Neither could win outright, and both had enough standing that forcing it would have been ugly. What got written was a form of words they could both sign, and it could be signed precisely because it did not say who decides. Everybody read it and everybody understood it differently, which was the point.

That ambiguity is not a hole in the governance. It is the thing that let the work start at all.

Which is why clear governance is so much harder than it sounds. Clarifying who decides means reopening the argument the vagueness settled, and this time somebody loses. So what you get instead is a RACI. It goes round for comment. Everyone signs it. Nobody uses it. And the distribution list on the next contentious email is exactly as long as it was before.

I would also gently say that governance is a communication initiative. Same intervention, firmer noun, org chart attached. That is what makes it feel structural rather than like another workshop, and it is why it gets approved when the workshop does not.

There is a cheap test for whether it worked, and it does not require a survey.

Take the same class of email three months later and count the recipients. If the list has not got shorter, the document described an authority that was never actually transferred, and the people copying six extra names are not confused. They have read the room correctly.

That is the part worth keeping. A long CC list is not noise and it is not bad manners. It is a free, continuously updated, unusually honest map of which decisions your organisation has not made yet.

Bad news is data, and so is an email header. Usually a more reliable one than the governance document sitting next to it.
