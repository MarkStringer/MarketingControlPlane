The complicated versus complex distinction is right and I use it. My problem is with the moment the classification happens, and with who is holding the pen.

You classify at the start. But the defining property of a complex project is that you cannot make out its shape from inside it, and the start is the furthest inside it you will ever be. You find out you were in the complex quadrant by having been wrong about which quadrant you were in. So the framework asks for the one judgement the situation is least equipped to supply.

That is the smaller problem. Here is the bigger one.

Your two axes do not get scored the same way.

Technical intricacy gets an honest number. Everybody enjoys saying the work is hard. It flatters the engineers, it justifies the budget, and nobody is embarrassed by it.

The people and politics axis never gets an honest number. Scoring that one high means writing down, on a document the sponsor will read, that the sponsor is the main source of unpredictability on their own programme. Nobody does that in month one of a job they would like to still have in month six. I have not done it. I have sat in that meeting, thought exactly that, and given the box a three.

So the framework reliably returns complicated. Not because most projects are complicated, but because the complex reading is unsayable in the room where the scoring happens. The instrument is fine. The room is the problem.

Point of view is worth 80 IQ points, and the point of view you need here is the one belonging to someone whose next contract does not depend on the answer. Score the politics axis with people who have nothing to lose, keep it off the circulated deck, and revisit it every month, because unlike the technical axis it moves.

Right-fit planning is the correct idea. It just needs one input that most organisations are structured to never produce.
