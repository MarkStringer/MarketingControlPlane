The relabelling point is right and it is the part most change writing gets wrong. Calling it resistance ends the enquiry, because once you have a word for why people are not doing it you stop looking. And the specificity move is right too. Adopt the CRM is not a behaviour. Run the Monday pipeline review off the CRM view instead of asking six people for a spreadsheet is a behaviour, and you can actually do something about it.

It is your five comparison questions I want to pick at, because I do not think they are five of a kind.

Which is easier in the real workflow. Which is faster under pressure. Those are design problems. Fewer clicks, a shorter form, a view that loads before the meeting starts. You can fix those with a good week and somebody who knows the tool.

Which is safer socially sits somewhere in the middle.

Which one is more rewarded, and which one do leaders actually reinforce, are not design problems at all.

If the spreadsheet is more rewarded, that is not friction and it is not inertia. Somebody is rewarding it. Usually because the spreadsheet lets a manager present a number they can shape before anyone else sees it, and the CRM view does not. That is not a bug they have failed to notice. It is the feature they are paying for.

Now look at your five steps. Specify, hypothesise, intervene, facilitate, test. Every one of those is something you can do on your own authority. None of them touches what a sales director is measured on at the end of the quarter.

So the method works beautifully right up to the boundary of what the person running the change is allowed to change, and everything on the far side of that boundary gets filed as friction.

Which is fine, as long as you say it out loud. Because when the honest answer to question four is the old behaviour, you have not found a behaviour design problem. You have found an unsettled argument about what the change is actually for, between people who outrank you, and no amount of reducing clicks is going to settle it. All projects are swamps, and that is the specific swamp this one is in.

The person still sending the spreadsheet is not confused and has not misunderstood the memo. They have read the reward system accurately, probably more accurately than the people who commissioned the change. Point of view is worth 80 IQ points, and theirs is the cheapest good intelligence in the building.

Your comparison is a genuinely useful instrument. Worth being upfront that on a bad day it will tell you something the project has no authority to fix.
