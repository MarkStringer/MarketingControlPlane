The dependency point is the strongest thing here and it does not get said enough. Buy the thinking often enough and you stop being able to do it, and the people who could have done it read the room and go somewhere they will be asked.

I want to push on the diagnosis though, because I think you land on it and then walk past it.

You say firms do this because they lack the confidence to trust their own people and to risk their own reputation. Agreed. But then look at what is actually being purchased, because it is not analysis and it is very often not even a point of view.

The point of view is usually already in the building.

Somebody two or three levels down has held it for eighteen months. They have said it in meetings. They may have written it up. It is not a secret and it is not subtle. What that person does not have is standing, and standing is the actual product on the invoice.

A point of view is worth eighty IQ points. It is the single cheapest thing an organisation can acquire and most of them have several lying around unread. What costs money is being allowed to hold one out loud, in front of a board, with nobody able to reply that you would say that, wouldn't you.

The consultancy supplies two things your own person structurally cannot. Standing, and the ability to leave afterwards. Those are not intellectual services. They are political ones, and they are priced accordingly.

Which brings me to the firing.

If the incentive really is what you describe, then being wrong on your own ends a career and being wrong alongside a brand name does not, and dismissing the person who hired McKinsey does not touch that incentive at all. It just adds a new one. The next person in that chair still needs the cover, still cannot survive being wrong unaccompanied, and now knows to buy it in a way that is harder to see. You will get shorter engagements, vaguer scopes, and a lot of advisory work booked under other headings.

To fix it you would have to change what happens to someone who is wrong by themselves. Not celebrate failure, none of that. Just make it survivable to be the named person on a call that did not come off. That is a real change, it costs real seniority, and it is much less satisfying than a dismissal.

There is one more thing in the invoice worth reading.

If a company has to pay several million to be told something a dozen of its own people already believe, the finding is not the strategy. The finding is that the reporting line cannot carry it. Bad news is data, but only in an organisation where the person delivering it survives contact with the person receiving it. Where that is not true, the news has to be laundered through a third party first, and the fee is the laundering charge.

So I would not fire them for hiring McKinsey. I would ask them which of their own people had already said it, and how long ago, and what happened to that person.

That answer is cheaper than the engagement and it tells you more.
