This is right, but I think it runs the causation backwards.

The reason dependency modelling ends up in the PM's head rather than in a tool isn't primarily that organisations picked the wrong software. It's that explicitly modelling dependencies also makes constraints and trade-offs explicit. If your tool shows that Task B can't start until Task A is done, it also shows who is responsible for Task A and what happens to the deadline if they're late. That's politically inconvenient.

A tool that makes the project legible also makes the politics legible. So the organisation ends up with a task tracker, and a person who holds the real model in their head — quietly, deniably, without receipts.

The tool problem is usually downstream of a decision not to know.
