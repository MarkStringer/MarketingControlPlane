The observation is right, but the causal story runs backwards.

Requirements look adequate at kickoff because you haven't started the project yet. The gaps, contradictions, and wrong assumptions only become visible once the work starts telling you what it actually is.

By the time you know enough to write truly stable requirements, you've already learned most of what the project was trying to teach you.

The 30x cost of late defect repair is real. But the issue isn't that requirements were written poorly — it's that requirements document what you believe about the work before you've done any of it, and the work tends to invalidate some of those beliefs. Better requirements writing is good. Believing that good requirements can stay stable long enough to build against is where the trouble starts.

Bad news from the project is data. Requirements are what you thought before you had it.
