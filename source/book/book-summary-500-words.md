# Delivering the Impossible — 500-word summary

Delivering the Impossible starts from a simple observation: most software projects that fail don't fail because of bad code. They fail because of a bad way of seeing the project in the first place. Point of view is worth eighty IQ points, and most of the tools project managers reach for, the Gantt charts, the fixed scopes, the sign off meetings, quietly make people worse at seeing what is actually happening.

The book argues that every software project is really two projects running at once. There is the idea value stream, how a proposal gets attention, funding and support, and that stream is weird: ideas get backed for emotional and political reasons, not because anyone has tested them. Then there is the product value stream, the thing the team actually has to build, which does not exist yet and has to be discovered. Most of the pain in a project comes from the gap between those two streams.

That gap opens up because projects happen in a swamp, not on solid ground. A mess of stakeholders, regulators, hidden constraints and competing interests influence the project. And no plan drawn up in a meeting room can anticipate them. The only way through is research, and the only way to make research honest is to get working software in front of real users as soon as possible. Even when it is embarrassing. That shifts a project from "push", where sponsors and specifications drive everything, to "pull", where user demand starts doing the steering.

Working software also surfaces the "pirate ship": the obvious problem on the horizon that everyone in the room can see and nobody wants to mention. This is because they know that raising the obvious issues will cause problems. Bosses often resist bad news because it threatens their own credibility, not caring so much what the effect will be on the project. Another way of seeing it: "bad news is data". Treating it that way, instead of as an attack, is one of the book's central arguments, aimed as much at bosses as at project managers.

Every project is a bet, and most of the machinery built around one, the methodologies, the certifications, the governance boards, is project management theater dressed up to make an uncertain bet look safe. The honest response is not to pretend the risk away. It is to break one big bet into many small ones through incremental delivery, and sometimes to admit the odds are bad enough that the project needs more money, more time, or a serious rethink rather than a quiet failure.

Underneath many if not all the problems that projects experience is one habit of mind the book keeps coming back to: symmetrical thinking, the unconscious assumption that a project should deliver all of it, exactly as first imagined, forever. Real delivery is asymmetrical: some features, for some people, for a while, close enough. The biscuit tin is not the biscuit. Agile, done properly, is a structured way of injecting some into people's desire for all.

None of this lets project managers off the hook, and it doesn't let bosses off it either. The book ends where it starts: all projects are swamps, every project is a bet, and the job is not to make the swamp disappear. It is to see it clearly enough to deliver the possible, instead of the fantasy.
