# Talking About The Show — Structure Notes

*Extracted from TalkingAboutTheShow.odt voice notes, May 2026*

## Core gimmick / opening activity

Give audience members notebooks and pens, ask them to write something, then pass it to someone else for comment. By the end of that exercise, they've done three stages of writing: write → edit/review → get feedback. The show *enacts* what it's teaching.

## Themes / set-pieces (roughly in order mentioned)

1. **The shitty first draft** (stolen from Anne Lamott, *Bird by Bird*) — just write something, anything. Get the internal editor to shut up.

2. **The 1-inch picture frame** (also Lamott) — write about one very small, specific thing. Don't try to write the whole thing.

3. **Tricking yourself into writing** — Douglas Adams "flying = throwing yourself at the ground and missing." You have to silence the editor to write; then you have to switch the editor back on to land.

4. **How to Read a Book** (Mortimer Adler) — you don't have to read linearly; same applies to *writing*. You can do detail first, then structure, or structure first, then detail. Demolishes the house-building metaphor ("lay the foundations first").

5. **Writerly debt** — the NaNoWriMo experiment. Got 12 days in, realised he had no plot, characters with forgotten names, and an unmapped maze-library. Had to stop and tidy up. Debt = you go fast, skip tidying, then can't proceed without it. (Christmas wrapping paper metaphor.)

6. **Tell me a story** — from a literary agent at a reading event. We already *know* how to tell stories — we do it every day without self-consciousness. We have multiple voices depending on audience (stranger, police, loved one). The show voice question: who are you telling this to?

7. **Planners vs pantsers** — the woman at the event who needs full structure before starting vs. James Ellroy's enormous concordance method vs. writing fragments and letting the concordance emerge. Both valid.

8. **Undercooked manuscripts** — the slush-pile reader who said most rejected books aren't bad, they're *unfinished*. The craft question is: how do you know when it's done?

9. **The writing partner** — reading each other's work for 2–3 hours a week broke a years-long editing block. The key move that got the book finished.

10. **Emotional labour** (Arlie Russell Hochschild) — feeling despair but going on anyway is *work*. Getting stuck and stopping is the natural response; not stopping is the difficult skill.

11. **The 2023 Edinburgh experiment** — no venue, did the show three times a day in the Meadows anyway, stole a venue for one night, got COVID in week 2. The point: rehearsing without a venue meant being ready when the chance came.

12. **The personal arc** — 57 years old, wife says "you haven't always wanted to be a writer," but actually has been writing since 14–15, just in the wrong register (diaries, starts abandoned at sticking points, side quests).

## Structural shape suggested by the content

The show could mirror the writing process itself:

- **Open** with the audience exercise (write → share → get feedback) — make them writers immediately
- **Middle** sections follow the journey: the tricks to start, the debt trap, the voices question, the planning problem, the "is it done?" problem
- **End** with the emotional labour / persistence argument and the 2023 Edinburgh story as proof

Mark's own note: *"I don't have an ending for this show yet, but I don't think that's bad for a first go through."*
