# You Can Write a Book: what the show says

A one-person Edinburgh Fringe 2026 show by Mark Stringer, author of *Delivering the Impossible* (Apress/Springer). It is about getting stuck while writing a book and getting unstuck. It runs on a deck of cards that all begin with S, taken in random order (picked by the audience when there is one), plus two optional live writing exercises and a set of "heckle" cards at the end. Because the order varies, this document groups the material by topic, not by performance.

## 1. The frame

### The four questions (opening)
Taken from a friend's non-fiction course (closing night credits Professor Richard Toye): an introduction should answer *Why me? Why you? Why this? Why now?* Used in every performance.

- **Why me:** He wrote a real book with a real publisher: *Delivering the Impossible*, on project management. He has been a project manager for a long time.
- **Why you:** If you want to write a book, you've come to the right place. (Closing night: "if anybody had turned up".) On 25 Aug the one audience member had a nearly finished book.
- **Why this:** The show is about getting stuck and the ways he got unstuck. He contrived for every idea to begin with S. It began as "Please Buy My Book" before he realised that wasn't a show.
- **Why now:** The claim that nobody needs to write books because AI will do it.

### Why now: the AI argument
Closing night: the claim is "bullshit" in the technical sense (said by people who don't care if it's true). The AI companies tell bosses to buy AI so they can employ fewer people, and tell creators to despair so that creators will sell cheaply. In the other performances he opens with a friend's remark that he's "just providing training material for the AI engines", and answers with a Sartre line (wording uncertain): *there may have been better times, but these are ours.* You can't write like the Romantic poets or the Victorians, so this is the time you have. The same argument returns in the "AI to steal" heckle.

## 2. The S cards

### Stuck
The point of the show. He wrote the bulk of a book, then stalled. On closing night he says he has realised he is stuck again, on a mystery story, and that part of the reason for the show is to get himself unstuck. On 26 Aug he gives details: about 35,000 words in, too many elements (a chapel of antiquities, a bookshelf maze, gold, an unsolved murder, an explosion). His method that morning was to write down "what's wrong with this".

### Subconscious
Writing is a discussion, or "dance", with the subconscious. The blues line "if it's in you it's going to come out" is attributed to Muddy Waters or B.B. King, with the joke that anything you think someone said probably wasn't said by them. His first version of the show used a stack of books as slides. He left the suitcase of them on the London Underground and reads that as his subconscious saying the format was too straightforward. The Underground, he suggests, is the subconscious of lots of Londoners. (24 Aug has the line as the Victoria line.)

### Sulk
The core story. He commuted Limehouse to Uxbridge (1h45 on the Metropolitan line) and decided to write every morning, as the how-to-write books advised. After a month he had 20,000 words and no idea what to do with them. His expectations came from a Hollywood montage: typing, seasons changing, manuscript growing. When it didn't happen he sulked for six years. Had he persevered he might be on his third book. The 26 Aug and 25 Aug versions date the start to May 2016 (uncorrected, treat as uncertain). He found the six-year gap by reading his collected diaries.

### Shitty
From Anne Lamott's *Bird by Bird* (the Audubon "bird by bird" story is told in garbled form on every night, and the closing-night transcript notes Lamott tells it about her father and brother). All first drafts are shitty. Hemingway is quoted to the same effect in several shows. Because "shitty" can make people avoid the draft, he prefers "half-arsed" and then "a tenth of an arse". The basis is an article by a man who read a publisher's unsolicited submissions (the slush pile) for a year, about 100 manuscripts. His finding was that the books weren't wrong; they were unfinished, at different levels of finishedness: language, logic, small and large structures. A first draft is still something you've made, and the job is to take it from a tenth of an arse to a whole one.

### Start
Mortimer Adler's *How to Read a Book* (1940) says you needn't read from start to end: use the contents, read the conclusion first, read only the war bits of *War and Peace*. His forehead-slapping realisation was that writing needn't be linear either. Start in the middle and tunnel out, or write the introduction or conclusion. He met a woman at a literary event who couldn't start until she had a full outline, and suspects that is an excuse. Wodehouse and Ellroy (his "concordance") are cited as full-outline writers on 22, 24 and 25 Aug. If you're stuck, do another piece: find another island you're comfortable on.

### Surprise (emotions and emotional work)
The six universal emotions, readable on any face, including the isolated Andaman Islands tribe who kill missionaries (you can tell they're angry). On 22 Aug it was run as an audience quiz. From Arlie Russell Hochschild's *The Managed Heart*: cabin crew, doctors and defence lawyers feel one emotion and must show another, and that's work you're paid for. His point is that doing something new brings negative emotions and you must deal with them, not avoid the thing that causes them, or you give up. Very little talk about perseverance covers this. On closing night he applies it to himself ("I'm a little bit upset" that nobody came). Anecdote on 24 and 25 Aug: a fear of the technical rehearsal for his first Fringe show, which turned out to be lights and music.

### Story
At an East London literary event in a bookshop (writers sent in first lines), a published novelist and agent was asked how to heighten the language. Her answer: *tell me a story.* His reading: when telling a story you don't fret about language, and everyone can tell stories (to friends, parents, colleagues, the police when there's a body under the patio). Project management is a dry subject, so he needed a story about the ideas as well as war stories. Once the stories come from similar voices at a similar pitch, you have found a voice. Voice comes late, not first. The 26 Aug show adds a proposal-writing book saying non-fiction must be entertaining.

### Single place
Keep everything you write in one place (or two: one for handwriting, one backed up for digital). That's how he could pick up the 20,000 words after six years. On 24 Aug he adds Agatha Christie, whose work was spread across 14 notebooks (the Val McDermid account is uncertain). On 26 Aug he cites Gerald Weinberg's *Fieldstone Method*. He admits he isn't as good at this as he'd like.

### Split infinitive
From *The Language Wars*: grammar books are often about ethnicity, nationality and class, and aim to stop people using grammar the author dislikes. The split-infinitive rule comes from Latin, where an infinitive is one word. "To boldly go" is fine in English. If you can speak the language you can write it, and you're in the club. For polish, Word, Grammarly, or asking an AI to list errors without correcting them (one of the few places AI is useful), or paying a proofreader (as he did). Hunter S. Thompson is said to have retyped passages of favourite authors. He floats a show for next year, *Grammar For People Who Aren't Twats*, and Muphry's Law (correcting others' grammar produces mistakes of your own).

### Skull, not crossbones
Publishing with a real publisher means losing control. He disliked the cover (a man running into machinery, icons in cogs, an AI/computer-chip icon). He won a martini glass (the martini principle: start mixing a martini and someone will tell you you're doing it wrong), the biscuit (his earlier show *The Biscuit Tin and the Biscuit*: the description is not the thing), and dice for the Bet chapters. He asked for a skull and crossbones for the pirate-ship chapter ("Ship") and got a skull. A month of arguing over the contract changed only the date format from US to UK. Don't get stuck on it. On 25 Aug he adds the $800 payment and US tax form and says arguing over the contract made his first three chapters late (Society of Authors reviewed it).

### Scene, not genius
A friend who loves music wouldn't take guitar lessons because he didn't want to learn to read music: "Paul McCartney doesn't read music." Mark: you are not a genius, and he had discovered the same about himself with the 20,000 words, and sulked. The idea is Brian Eno's "scenius". Liverpool in the 1960s had American blues records and young people with money on a Saturday night. His own scene was small: working from home and from Greece (a two-hour time difference gave him four hours to write each morning before work), and swapping chapters with someone. Virginia Woolf's *A Room of One's Own*: you need money, time and space. The question to ask: how can you improve your scene? Present on 22 Aug and closing night; absent in the 24, 25 and 26 Aug transcripts.

### Scott (talk your way out of it)
Added to the deck by 26 Aug. An explorer inspired by the film *Scott of the Antarctic* attempted a polar record unaided and went bankrupt. He wrote to John Mills blaming him. Mills invited him round for tea, said "you'll have to talk your way out of it", and they spent hours in the bathroom writing an inspirational talk. That began an after-dinner speaking career. (24 Aug has it as a Radio 4 story.) Mark's parallel: at the 2023 Fringe he had no venue, so he performed half-hour talks on the Meadows up to three times a day, recording each. When a friend with a topical comedy show got Covid, Mark took the slot, told the audience it would be about project management, and nobody left (around 18 people, per 26 Aug). Talking is "editing by talking", which helps because he finds editing prose hard. He suggests this is the oral tradition and wonders whether it's a method for editing a book.

### Stay in the pool
Closing night only. Colm Tóibín, seen in a video that day, says writing is like swimming: some days your back hurts and you want to get out, and the job is staying in the pool. Mark failed to do that with the first 20,000 words and now has 35,000 words of a mystery, so must stay in and stop paddling in the shallow end.

## 3. The live exercises (when there is an audience)

1. **One-inch frame.** From *Bird by Bird*: write for about two minutes on something from the last 24 hours, as seen through a one-inch frame. Mark writes too. Debrief: people struggle to think of anything, one person drew a frame, and on 24 Aug the group included slow processing and bad handwriting. His point: writing anything is the most important step.
2. **Reread and edit.** Reread and change it in any way. Audience reports: less scary with something to work from, one changed tense, one changed viewpoint (street to window), one finds revision harder than writing. Mark says editing is his hardest part, and that writing and editing, repeated, are the two most important steps.

Skipped on closing night (no audience).

## 4. Finding a reader (24, 25, 26 Aug)
Don't ask friends or family to read unfinished work. During the pandemic he posted draft chapters online and someone he barely knew proposed swapping chapters. They have swapped weekly for about four years, at roughly two hours a week. The partner coached him on proposals and pointed him to the publisher that took his. (The partner suffered a family bereavement and a stroke during this; the transcripts are vague, so leave this out of anything public unless Mark confirms.)

## 5. Heckle cards (every show)
Mark hands them out; when nobody is there he reads them himself. He says audiences love them.

- **"You're just writing content for AI to steal."** Answered with the AI argument above. Closing night adds Cory Doctorow's book (title forgotten on tape): the AI companies want you scared and in despair, so don't be, or feel the fear and do it anyway. Elsewhere: writing is a dance with the subconscious and a negotiation with your emotions.
- **"Boo."** Not everybody will like what you do. A reviewer said they'd like to see his next book (a "jiu-jitsu insult"). Some reviewers wouldn't post their review on Amazon because he wouldn't like it.
- **"This is shit."** Several people won't like it; deal with it.
- **"Tell us a joke."** Closing night: "fur coat, no knickers" (a polar bear). Other nights: the Buddha line "we become the thing we must resist", with a fat-Yorkshireman punchline; on 26 Aug a "no time like the present" joke.
- **"Why should I listen to you?"** A psychologist's idea: we learn fastest from someone about 18 months ahead of us. He has written one book and has a lot to learn, but is that far ahead of someone who hasn't started.

## 6. The close
Varies by night. Closing night: he plans to practise the show before next August so it's better, and he absolutely must get better at marketing. Other nights: thanks to the audience and an invitation to keep the notepads. On 25 Aug the one audience member said it was "extremely enjoyable".

## 7. Differences worth knowing

- **Order is random.** 24 Aug opens with Start, Single place and Story; 25 Aug opens with the emotions card; 26 Aug opens with Scott and Split infinitive; closing night opens with Subconscious.
- **Cards that come and go.** *Scene not genius* appears on 22 Aug and closing night only. *Stay in the pool* and the *Tube suitcase* are only on closing night (and the suitcase on 24 Aug). *Scott* is on 24, 25, 26 Aug and closing night but not 22 Aug. Doctorow is only on closing night.
- **Not in the final show:** the earlier Kübler-Ross structure in `show-running-order-kubler-ross.md` (Death, Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance, then "the dangerous thing") does not appear in any recorded performance. The S-card format replaced it.
