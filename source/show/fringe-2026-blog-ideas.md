# Blog post ideas from three recordings of the same show

Three performances, same set of S-cards, drawn in a different order each night.
Because the order changes, the same material gets told differently — and the
differences are where most of these ideas come from.

All timestamps refer to the transcripts in this directory. All quotations are
from **machine transcripts** and must be checked against the audio before use.

---

## 1. The show is a crowbar (strongest of the set)

**The material.** Mid-run, walking along a street in Edinburgh, Mark works out
why he is doing the show at all. He has 35,000 words of a mystery story and he
is stuck — the same way he was stuck at 20,000 words in 2016, the stuck that
turned into a six-year sulk. He is doing a show about getting unstuck because he
is stuck.

> "I was walking along the streets and I was like, oh, yes. I'm stuck with my
> book. And I'm doing a show about being stuck with books. Maybe those two
> things are connected." — 22 Aug, [38:41]

> "I think kind of part of the reason I'm doing this show is because I'm writing
> another book and I'm stuck. I only kind of realized that midway through the run
> last week." — 25 Aug, [51:03]

**Why it works as a post.** It is the confessional register the style guide is
built around, and it lands the show's own thesis on the person giving it. It
also earns the insight rather than stating it: the story arrives first, the
point arrives second.

**Shape.** Open on the street. The awful realisation. Then back to May 2016, the
Metropolitan Line, the thousand words a day, the 20,000 words, the six years.
Then: this time the sulk gets a venue and a poster and a run of tickets.

**Ties to.** `source/show/talking-about-the-show-structure-notes.md`, the John
Mills material below.

## 2. An audience of one

**The material.** On 25 August one person came. Mark offered to call it off.

> "Did you want me to go away and you can go home and have a night off?"
> "No, that's absolutely fine." — [00:03]

He did the whole show. The cards, the two-minute writing exercise, the editing
exercise, the heckles — read out by himself, to one person, because there was
nobody else to read them. The audience member had a book of their own, nearly
finished, and turned out to be better at editing than he is. At the end:

> "Well, I hope it was worthwhile with just one person." / "It was extremely
> enjoyable." — [55:04]

**Why it works as a post.** This is the Fringe that people don't put on posters,
and it maps exactly onto the book's argument: you do the work whether or not the
conditions are ideal. There is also a nice inversion — the show is about showing
your unfinished work to a stranger, and that night the audience did exactly that
back at him.

**Shape.** Don't editorialise. Describe the room, the offer to go home, the
notebook, the pen, the two minutes of silence with one person writing. End on
the exchange above. Resist adding a moral.

**Careful.** Don't identify the audience member, and don't quote what they wrote.

## 3. I left the books on the Victoria line

**The material.** An earlier version of the show was built around a suitcase of
books — hold one up, talk about it, next book. He describes it as "devolving
into a PowerPoint presentation." Then he left the suitcase on the Underground.

> "Some part of me decided that that wasn't the way to do this show and left
> those books on the Victoria line. Were they destroyed in the controlled
> explosion? I don't know." — 24 Aug, [43:11]

**Why it works as a post.** A very short, very funny piece about editing by
accident, and about the subconscious removing a crutch you weren't willing to
put down. The London Underground shows up twice in the story of this book — the
Metropolitan Line is where it got written, the Victoria line is where the props
went. That symmetry is free.

**Shape.** Under 300 words. The suitcase, the loss, the shrug, the better show.
The controlled-explosion line is the ending.

## 4. Nobody warns you that the second job is emotional

**The material.** Arlie Russell Hochschild's *emotional labour* — cabin crew,
doctors, defence lawyers, all paid to feel one thing and display another. Mark's
move: when you start writing, you hand yourself that second job too, and nobody
mentions it in the books about writing.

> "Responding to emotions is work. So you have this idea of like you're going to
> write a book and you've got a Hollywood idea of writing a book... but in fact
> what happens is when you start to write a book you send yourself on this
> emotional journey." — 22 Aug, [42:08]

Paired with the tech rehearsal story, which is the same point at human scale: he
nearly didn't do his first Fringe show because he didn't know what a tech
rehearsal was and was frightened of looking stupid in front of theatre people.

> "How do you want your lights? ... Do you want to play some music? ... And that
> was it." — 25 Aug, [08:47]

**Why it works as a post.** Straight transfer to project management, which is
the audience the book is for. Every project hands people work they don't know how
to do, and the response is usually to avoid the room rather than the task.

**Shape.** Hochschild first, briefly. Then the tech rehearsal. Then the turn:
this is what a project does to people every week, and it's why they go quiet.

## 5. They weren't bad. They weren't finished.

**The material.** The would-be writer who asked a publisher to let him read the
slush pile instead of sending them a manuscript, and read a hundred of them. What
separated the publishable from the unpublishable wasn't prose quality:

> "The main difference... was that the unpublishable ones weren't finished. They
> might be in the right length, but they hadn't had enough effort put into them."
> — 25 Aug, [11:31]

Hence the reframe: not "shitty first draft" but a tenth of an arse. Not bad — not
yet paid for.

**Why it works as a post.** `2026-03-27-the-shitty-first-draft` already covered
the Lamott/Hemingway framing. This is a genuinely different claim: the failure
mode isn't quality, it's abandonment, and that's a much more useful thing to know
because it tells you what to do next.

**Careful.** Mark can't remember the author or the publication of that article.
Either find it or write it as "I read an article by someone who" — don't invent
an attribution.

## 6. Don't ask the people who love you to read it

**The material.** Four years of Saturdays with a man he had met once at a meetup
before the pandemic, who saw draft chapters posted online and offered to swap.
Two hours a week. Through it: the man's wife died, and he had a stroke.

> "We kind of Englished our way through it. Hello, how are you? Oh, terrible.
> Okay, well then shall we look at the draft?" — 24 Aug, [51:13]

And the advice that comes out of it:

> "I don't think it's fair to ask your nearest and dearest to read the stuff
> that you've written... they're used to reading finished stuff. They don't have
> any kind of handle on reading stuff that's not finished." — 24 Aug, [51:54]

**Why it works as a post.** The second quotation is the most immediately useful
sentence in all three recordings and it isn't anywhere in the blog yet. The
friendship is the evidence for it, and it's genuinely moving without being
worked up.

**Careful.** Do not name the friend or give details of his wife's death or his
stroke without asking him first. It can be written entirely as "the man I swap
chapters with."

## 7. How I know I had a real publisher: I'm furious about the cover

**The material.** The running joke of the run, and it lands every night. He asked
for a skull and crossbones for the pirate-ship chapter; they printed the skull
and dropped the crossbones. There's a man running over cogs that nobody can
explain. He spent a month having the Society of Authors go through the contract,
took their objections to the publisher, and won exactly one concession:

> "The only thing that they changed was the date format from US date format to
> UK date. That was my victory." — 25 Aug, [22:22]

And the payoff, on a book about project management:

> "Because I'd spent a month arguing over contract, my first interaction with my
> editor was her saying, well, your first three chapters are late." — 25 Aug, [22:35]

Plus the $800 and the terrifying American tax form required to collect it.

**Why it works as a post.** Funny, specific, and it's a real answer to the
question everyone asks authors ("did you self-publish?"). The late-chapters
punchline is the best joke in the three recordings and it is about project
management.

**Shape.** The question, the joke answer ("I know it was a real publisher because
of how angry I am about the cover"), then the evidence, ending on the late
chapters.

## 8. Why you should listen to someone eighteen months ahead of you

**The material.** Answering his own planted heckle, "Why should I listen to you?"

> "Apparently, children learn fastest from somebody who's about 18 months older
> than them. So if you've not written a book... I'm just a little bit ahead of
> you. I'm not a seasoned professional. I don't know all the answers." — 25 Aug, [53:27]

(On 22 Aug the same bit ends: "which for me makes me feel terrible for my little
brother.")

**Why it works as a post.** It's a defence of writing from just ahead rather than
from the summit, which is the position most people who could write a useful book
are actually in — and it doubles as an argument for reading a book by someone who
has written one book rather than twelve.

**Careful.** He attributes this to a developmental psychologist he used to know
and hedges it in performance ("my children at least"). Keep the hedge or drop the
claim to anecdote.

## 9. More by luck than management

**The material.** Asked how he got unstuck, the honest answer is a stack of
accidents: the pandemic; working from home; therefore working from Greece; the
two-hour time difference giving him a morning; posting chapters online;
a near-stranger offering to swap; that stranger already having a publisher.

> "All of those things which I feel queasy saying it, but I think we're just kind
> of fortunate more than anything else. Like good fortune got me out of this
> sulk." — 25 Aug, [45:50]

His dad's phrase for it: more by luck than management.

**Why it works as a post.** The queasiness is the post. It's an author of a
project management book admitting that the thing that unblocked his own
multi-year project was not management. Sits naturally next to "the project is a
bet."

**Optional second half.** Brian Eno's *scenius*, and Woolf on money and a room —
the argument that you can't manufacture luck but you can build the scene it lands
in. Note that `2026-03-29-you-are-not-paul-mccartney` used the McCartney half of
this card already; the scene half is unused.

## 10. The same show, three times, in a different order

**The material.** The cards are drawn by the audience, so the running order is
different every night.

> "That story will unfold and it unfolds in a different way every night because I
> talk about the different bits of it in a different order every night just to
> keep it interesting. I think that's why I'm doing it that way anyway." — 25 Aug, [02:28]

And on the cards themselves:

> "I've contrived them all to begin with S. And the result of contriving for them
> all to begin with S was that when I went back to them, some of them had
> forgotten." — 25 Aug, [04:19]

He has a card that just says "Split" and has to work out on stage what he meant.

**Why it works as a post.** A show with no fixed order, about a book you don't
have to write in order, given by someone who can't reconstruct his own notation.
The three recordings are the evidence: same material, three shapes, and the
version given to one person is a different thing entirely from the version given
to a full room.

**Shape.** This one only works if it's written after the run ends, looking back
at all of them.

# Verification checklist before publishing any of these

  Don't name him without a source.
