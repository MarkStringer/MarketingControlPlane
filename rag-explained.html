RAG: Two Explanations

Two answers on Retrieval-Augmented Generation, compiled from our conversation. The first explains what RAG is from first principles; the second explains how it fits with knowledge graphs, grounded in this repo's scripts/ask_graph.py.

1. What RAG is

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. It's a technique for making a language model answer using specific information it wasn't trained on — your documents, your database, your repo — by fetching the relevant material at question time and putting it into the prompt.

The problem it solves. A language model only knows two things: what it absorbed during training (frozen, general, and with a cutoff date), and whatever you type into the prompt right now. So out of the box it can't answer "what does our cadence policy say about bad-news posts?" — that's not in its training. And if you force it to, it tends to hallucinate: invent a plausible-sounding answer. RAG fixes this by feeding the model the actual source text before it answers.

The mechanism — three steps. The name itself is the recipe:

  1. Retrieval. Take the user's question, search some external knowledge store for the most relevant pieces of text, and pull back the top handful. The search is usually semantic: documents are pre-converted into embeddings (vectors that capture meaning), the question is embedded the same way, and you return the chunks whose vectors are closest. ("Semantic" matters: it matches on meaning, so "bad news" can retrieve a passage about "delivering unwelcome project updates" even with no shared words.)
  2. Augmentation. Paste those retrieved chunks into the prompt as context — literally "Here is some background:\n\n[retrieved text]\n\nNow answer this question: ...".
  3. Generation. The model writes its answer, grounded in the supplied text rather than its own memory. A good RAG prompt also instructs it to use only the provided context and to say "I don't know" if the answer isn't there.

What it's actually doing, in one line: turning a closed-book exam into an open-book one. The model stops reciting from memory and starts reading from the page you handed it.

Why people use it instead of alternatives:

  • vs. fine-tuning (retraining the model on your data): RAG is cheaper, and you can update the knowledge instantly by changing the documents — no retraining.
  • vs. dumping everything into the prompt: even huge context windows are finite and expensive; retrieval sends only the relevant slice.
  • It gives citations — because you know which chunks you retrieved, you can show the source, which makes answers checkable.

Its limits: the answer is only as good as the retrieval. If the search misses the right chunk, the model can't use it; if it retrieves junk, the answer degrades. And it answers from isolated retrieved snippets, so it's weak on questions that require connecting facts across many documents — which is exactly the gap a knowledge graph fills (GraphRAG, below). But that's a refinement of the retrieval step, not a change to the core idea.

Concretely in your repo: scripts/ask_graph.py is RAG. It retrieves relevant nodes and edges from your graph (the "R"), pastes them into the prompt as context (the "A"), and asks the model to answer using only that (the "G").

2. How RAG fits with knowledge graphs

This one fits tightly, and your repo already does it: scripts/ask_graph.py is a small GraphRAG engine. Let me use it to explain.

Plain RAG is: chunk your documents → embed them as vectors → at question time, retrieve the top-k most similar chunks → stuff them in the prompt → let the model answer. Its weakness is that it retrieves isolated chunks by similarity. It has no idea that chunk A grounds chunk B, it can't follow a chain of reasoning across documents, and it can't answer "what is this whole corpus about?" because no single chunk contains that.

A knowledge graph fixes exactly those gaps. It contributes two things to retrieval:

  1. The relationships become retrievable context, not just the text. You feed the model "A grounds B", "cadence constrains this post" — the typed edges themselves — alongside the node summaries.
  2. Neighbour expansion / multi-hop. Once you've found a relevant node, you follow its edges to pull in connected nodes that a pure similarity search would have missed.

You can watch both happen in answer_question (ask_graph.py:246):

  • rank_nodes (line 256) — find the seed nodes that match the question. (This is the "retrieval" step. Note it's lexical keyword scoring, line 105, not vector embeddings — so it's a simplified RAG, but the shape is identical.)
  • rank_edges (line 260) — score edges, with a +1.5 boost for any edge touching a seed node (line 145). The relationships get pulled in deliberately.
  • expand_with_neighbors (line 263) — this is the graph-specific move. It walks the top edges and adds the nodes on the other end, even though those nodes never matched the question's keywords. That's the multi-hop reach plain RAG lacks.
  • render_context (line 171) — serialises a NODES: block and an EDGES: block with type, weight, and evidence. The model receives the graph structure, not a bag of chunks.
  • ask_model (line 221) — restricts the model to that context.

So the answer to "how does RAG fit": a knowledge graph is a better retrieval substrate for RAG. Vector RAG retrieves things that look alike; GraphRAG retrieves things that are connected, plus the named connections themselves.

Two directions worth keeping straight, because your repo shows both:

  • Graph improves RAG (retrieval) — ask_graph.py, above.
  • RAG-style extraction builds the graph — the semantic graphify-out/ layer uses an LLM to read documents and emit the nodes and edges in the first place. The inverse relationship.

And the piece your repo has but ask_graph.py doesn't yet exploit: for global questions ("what are the main themes across everything?"), full GraphRAG (e.g. Microsoft's variant) retrieves pre-computed community summaries rather than chunks. Your GRAPH_REPORT.md already detects the 56 communities and the god nodes — so the raw material for that upgrade is sitting there. The honest caveats on the current script: retrieval is keyword-based (swapping in embeddings would sharpen it) and it expands only one hop (true multi-hop traversal would let it answer deeper chains).
