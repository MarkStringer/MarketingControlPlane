# Posts considered

## Selected

- **Usman Sheikh** — "Project Everest didn't fail. The partnership model killed it." (**1y**, 3,329 reactions, 330 comments) — SELECTED. By a wide margin the highest engagement post reached in this run or the last several, and unusually it earns it. A structured post mortem of EY's abandoned audit and consulting split, laid out as The Vision, The Math Made Sense, What Actually Happened and The Truth, arguing that the partner model rather than the plan killed it, because the people who must vote for change are the people most invested in the status quo. Selected because it is a genuine project post wearing business commentary clothes, and public post mortems of a real programme with a business case and a governance route are rare. The non-obvious addition is that the partner vote was not a risk to the project, it was the project. Everything in his Vision and Math sections is downstream of a vote that had to be won from people who lose by it, and none of those upstream numbers were ever in dispute. Second addition is that a question of that kind gets more expensive to ask the longer it goes unasked, because accumulated spend starts arguing on the plan's behalf and the meeting changes from is this going to pass to are you seriously suggesting we stop now.
- **Richard Russell** — "Nobody gets fired for hiring McKinsey. But they should!" (2y, 90 reactions, 37 comments) — SELECTED. A real argument with a mechanism and a prescription, and the prescription does not follow from the mechanism, which is the most useful shape a post can have for this purpose. He correctly identifies that the hire buys protection from being personally wrong, then proposes firing the hirer, which leaves that incentive completely intact. The reply takes his own diagnosis more seriously than he does. The addition is that what is bought is not analysis and usually not even a point of view, since the view is generally already in the building, held by someone with no standing. Standing is the product, plus the ability to leave afterwards, which is the thing no internal person can supply.
- **John Sills** — "You don't have a people problem, you have a process problem" (1y, 38 reactions, 16 comments) — SELECTED, lowest engagement of the three and selected on argument shape. A clean binary with a volume threshold attached, a worked example he supplies himself, the laptops locked away and the leaders walking round at six checking, and a desire paths analogy. Arguable in a specific rather than a general way, which is what the brief asks for. The addition is the third option the binary excludes: the process is not failing, it is succeeding at an unstated job, which is producing a rule and a culprit rather than compliance, and that is why widespread non compliance never triggers a redesign. Comes with a cheap test attached.

# Replies drafted

- `queue/reply-candidates/reply-candidate-2026-08-12-001-sheikh-the-vote-was-the-project.md` — concedes the diagnosis and argues it is still generous to the plan, then moves the load bearing point. His own post structure is the evidence: The Vision and The Math describe the value of the end state, and none of those numbers were ever in question, while the only genuinely uncertain quantity was whether several thousand partners would vote to reduce their own position. That was not a risk to the project, it was the project. Generalises to the habit of business cases, which accumulate certainty about the modellable parts, which are usually the parts nobody was arguing about, and go quiet about the single unpriced assumption everything sits on. Uses "the project is a bet" to make the sizing point, that you size a bet on what you are least sure of, and doing it the other way round produces a beautiful number describing a world where you have already won. Then the timing consequence, that the will they vote for this question is cheap early and ruinous late, because accumulated spend does the arguing and the meeting changes from is this going to pass to are you seriously suggesting we stop now. Explicitly disclaims knowing when EY asked it. Uses "bad news is data" with the qualifier that it is only data while it can still change something. Ends on the point that the partnership model was the ground, was visible from day one, and was the one part of the plan nobody costed, with "deliver the possible, not the fantasy" applied to the smaller duller version that would have been called timid and would have happened.
- `queue/reply-candidates/reply-candidate-2026-08-12-002-russell-what-you-are-buying-is-standing.md` — concedes the dependency argument as the strongest thing in the post, then argues he lands on the diagnosis and walks past it. The purchase is not analysis and usually not even a point of view, because the view is already in the building, held by someone two or three levels down who has been saying it for eighteen months. What they lack is standing, and standing plus the ability to leave afterwards is the actual product on the invoice, which makes it a political service priced accordingly. Uses "point of view is worth 80 IQ points" with the twist that a point of view is the cheapest thing an organisation can own and the expensive part is being permitted to hold one out loud without someone replying that you would say that, wouldn't you. Then the counterpoint to his prescription: if being wrong alone ends a career and being wrong beside a brand name does not, firing the hirer leaves that untouched and teaches the next person to buy the same cover under a vaguer heading. The real fix is making it survivable to be wrong unaccompanied, which costs seniority and is much less satisfying than a dismissal. Closes with the invoice as evidence, that paying millions for a finding a dozen of your own people already hold means the reporting line cannot carry bad news and the fee is the laundering charge. Ends on a question to ask instead of firing, which of your own people already said it, how long ago, and what happened to them.
- `queue/reply-candidates/reply-candidate-2026-08-12-003-sills-who-the-process-is-actually-for.md` — concedes the desire path and the volume heuristic, two people versus ninety, as better than most of what gets offered. Adds the third option: the process is not failing, it is working perfectly at a job nobody wrote down. Walks his own laptop example through what it actually produces, a written instruction, a record it was issued, and a six o'clock walk round that generates a name, all of which perform exactly as designed the moment a laptop goes missing. Compliance was never the deliverable, the rule and the culprit were, which is why the failure never triggers a redesign. Supplies a cheap test: wait until someone follows the process and the bad thing happens anyway, then listen to the first sentence in the room, because "we followed the process" means record and "then the process is wrong" means method. Second half applies "bad news is data" to the muddy diagonal, which is a free continuously updated survey with a hundred percent response rate and complete honesty because nobody knew they were answering, and to which the standard response is a fence rather than tarmac, since laying the path means somebody's drawing was wrong and that somebody outranks whoever noticed the mud. Ends on the blunter question of who the process is for, usually an auditor or a previous incident rather than the people being watched, and the line that the path across the grass is the honest survey and the fence is the org chart.
