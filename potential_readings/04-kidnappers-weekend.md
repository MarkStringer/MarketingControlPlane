# A Good Night Out in Port-au-Prince

*Source: Chapter Commit, lines 6719–6800*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

---

Chris Voss wrote a book about negotiation called "Never Split the Difference." In it he talks about his experiences negotiating with kidnappers in, among other places, Haiti. When Voss worked at the FBI, various political and economic developments made Haiti a hard place to live. And those hard circumstances led to a rash of kidnappings. It was Voss's job to help the Haitian police to negotiate with these kidnappers.

Initial demands for money from the kidnappers were sky-high. They were way beyond the means of the kidnap victims' families. For example, an initial demand might be $100,000.

Voss's strategy was to ask open questions. For example: "How do I know they're OK?" This is a way of making the kidnappers think. And it's also a way of keeping the conversation going without needing to make any concessions. Voss would also ask "How do you expect me to do that?" He would combine this with a strategy to move the ransom toward a price that he knew the families could pay.

Voss got experience of multiple hostage negotiations in Haiti. And he noticed something odd. There were far more kidnappings on a Monday morning. That was the most popular day of the week. And the kidnappers seemed to get keener and keener to resolve the negotiations by Friday. What was going on? Why were kidnappers keeping business hours?

Voss's Haitian colleagues explained it to him. The kidnappers really wanted to go out on a Saturday night. If they got the ransom by Friday, that's what it would get spent on. This brought Voss to a crucial realization. The real ransom was just a few thousand dollars — the price of a good night out in Haiti.

---

What does this mean for projects? It means that keeping the conversation going and asking open questions is as important, if not more important, than meeting a particular deadline.

Recently I was on a project that delivered successfully. We were initially told that there was no room for movement on either the budget, or the deadline for go live — it had to be on time and to budget!

Our strategy wasn't to argue with this. We talked. We had a weekly meeting with the person in charge of the project. We arranged the big items we needed to do on the project from left to right on a timeline. This allowed us to ask open questions.

*"We're here. What do you think we should do next? How do you think we can meet this deadline?"*

And we learned things. The customer told us that another supplier, whose opinion they trusted, had refused to bid for the project. The supplier had told them that the project couldn't be done for the budget. And they were right — the final cost of the project was about twice the initial estimate. We also learned that internally, the organization was nowhere near being ready to start using the new service. And we learned that there was more money to be spent, if it was clear that the project could deliver.

The budget? It wasn't the budget. The deadline? It wasn't the deadline. The commitment? No one could have kept it.
