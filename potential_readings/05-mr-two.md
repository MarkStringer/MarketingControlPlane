# Mr. Two

*Source: Chapter Car, lines 4025–4080*
*Estimated reading time: ~2.5 minutes*

---

The first Agile software development project that I worked on was for a publishing company. When I joined the project, it had been going for over a year and had cost many millions of pounds.

A few months before I joined, there had been a show and tell meeting. This was an international company, and it had offices in London and in New York.

This is the story that I heard about that meeting. One of the project managers said that he was going to share project progress. This was progress that had been made through "the backlog." The backlog is the Agile way of saying the list of all the requirements for the project. It's basically the project's "to-do list."

The project manager had a presentation with some slides. And there was a bit of messing about setting up the presentation — and then making sure that it was shared with the office in New York. Finally, everything was set up and the project manager could move to the slide that showed the progress of the project. He clicked on to the slide. In the middle of the slide was a giant "2".

I don't know how dramatic he was at this point. I don't know if he asked anybody in the room or on the phone if they could guess what the two meant? Two weeks to finish? Two months? Two years? In fact, the two was percent. Next to the giant two was a tiny % sign. Progress on the project, after six months and many millions of pounds, was two percent of all the work that was on the to-do list.

This is brutal transparency. And it probably came out of desperation. Having worked on that project, I can guess that someone had told the bosses in New York that the project would be finished by a certain date. Nobody had mentioned to the bosses the problems that were being found — problems that were slowing progress to a crawl. Also, my guess was that nobody had said "no" to the bosses. Ever. And that was why the list of requirements was tens of times as long as could ever be delivered.

The main result of that number "2" was the project manager was never allowed to speak at a show and tell again. The bosses in New York hired a "senior project manager" to communicate progress across the Atlantic. After that, guess what? The news was always good!

This is what one friend of mine said about that meeting:

> "I remember an explosion of anger over the phone where the entire team — fifty or more people — were dressed down and told to get on with it or heads will roll."

But here's the thing. Bad news is data. The "2" was the most useful thing that happened on that project. And it was punished out of existence.
