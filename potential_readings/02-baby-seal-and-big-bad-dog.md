# The Baby Seal and the Big Bad Dog

*Source: Chapter NotBet, lines 5576–5695*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

---

> Garry: "And now for the first item on the agenda: spoons."
>
> Bev: "Really? That's the most important thing for us to talk about? You're going to talk about spoons?"
>
> Garry: "Well, it was on the agenda last week and I've got an update."
>
> Bev: [Angrily rising from the table] "I can't believe this, I'm leaving."

This was a scene that played out one morning on a project I was working on. The project managers had spent several months trying to communicate to senior people about our problems. The response of the project's bosses had been to appoint a "senior project manager" and to tell us that we should communicate all our problems to him.

I sighed. I was every bit as furious and frustrated as Bev, and it could have just as easily been me who lost my temper. The program lead had given us a baby seal.

What's a baby seal? Let's say you're on a project and the news is bad, the news is very bad. The people charged with delivering the project — the project managers — are starting to shout quite loudly. They are trying to communicate to senior management that the news is bad. The response of senior management is to stop listening.

But the way that they stop listening is quite sophisticated. They appoint someone — often with an important-sounding title, such as "senior project manager." Then they say, "all communication needs to go through the senior project manager."

Here's the thing. The "senior project manager" is the gentlest, mildest-mannered person that you're ever going to meet. They have big, cute eyes and a gentle expression. Like a baby seal. You can't club the baby seal! Look at those big, cute eyes! The baby seal is completely conflict-averse. They are all soft edges. Arguing with them is like shouting at fog or punching pocket lint. They're never going to tell the senior stakeholders that there are problems with the project. The entire reason that they are there is to prevent that from happening.

Of course, some senior managers don't have the finesse that's required to appoint a baby seal. Rather than finding a gentle soul to completely muffle the sounds of trouble, they buy a big aggressive dog. At the slightest sniff of bad news, the dog growls and snarls and even tries to bite. The dog makes anyone who tries it regret their attempts to tell the truth.

Weirdly, in my experience, this is a less "successful" strategy than the baby seal for preventing the reporting of bad news. It doesn't stop news getting through as effectively. There are only so many times that someone can threaten to fire you. And the big bad dog is unlikely to have the authority to fire you. If they manage to arrange that, too many questions get asked. Even "Mr. Two" — the project manager who revealed that a project costing millions of pounds was only two percent complete — didn't get fired.
