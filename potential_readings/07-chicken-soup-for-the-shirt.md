# Chicken Soup for the Shirt

*Source: Chapter Boss, lines 9221–9310*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

---

I read a book called "Sleight of Mouth," by Robert Dilts. As an exercise in the book, Dilts asks the reader to try to understand the difference between ideas and reality.

He asks his readers to think of a thing that happened. And then he asks them to think of a thing that didn't happen — a thing that could have happened, but didn't. The two things should be of around the same level of interest. So, for example, if the thing that did happen was:

> "I had sugar in my coffee this morning,"

the thing that didn't happen shouldn't be:

> "I came to work by personal jet pack."

My two example sentences were:

> "I had chicken soup for lunch."

as the thing that happened. And:

> "I had a bacon sandwich for lunch."

as the thing that could have happened — but didn't.

The next stage of the exercise is to think about these two statements. How do you see, hear, feel or taste any difference between a true statement and one that could be true — but isn't?

I examined these two phrases in my mind's eye. And I tried to understand the difference between them. How did I know I had chicken soup for lunch and not a bacon sandwich?

Then I suddenly realized. There was a big difference.

> I spilled some of the soup on my shirt!

Thinking about that soup on my shirt was a big revelation. There is an important difference between ideas and reality. And it's this:

> In reality — stuff goes wrong.

When we do stuff, things will almost never go exactly the way that we expect them to. Unexpected things will happen. There will be things we've forgotten that will pop up to cause trouble. There will be other things we've never even thought of that we will have to deal with.

Projects that aren't happening don't have stuff going wrong. Projects that are happening? They have stuff going wrong.

Projects that are really happening generate all sorts of unexpected problems, questions and mistakes — like spillages on my shirt.

And this revelation has important implications. No news of stuff going wrong means either:

> A: The project isn't really happening.

Or:

> B: Nobody is telling you about what's going wrong.

If you're a boss — if you're in charge of a project — this is something you need to understand.
