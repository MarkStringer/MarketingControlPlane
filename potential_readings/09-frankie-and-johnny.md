# Frankie and Johnny

*Source: Chapter Commit, lines 6855–6920*
*Note: This passage references a prior scene (Johnny demanding a delivery commitment). It works best with a brief intro: "I was on a project. The boss — let's call him Johnny — was demanding that I promise the project would be delivered by a certain date. And when I refused, he told me that I had promised it."*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

What was *really* going on? What were the real hidden factors in this situation? I put them together later. I think there were two. The first was that Johnny had an upcoming meeting with his board of directors. The board had given him a moderately large amount of money — several million pounds — nearly two years earlier, and he had nothing yet to show for it. Most of the delay had been his mistake: he'd fallen a little too hard for the idea of "move fast and break things."

One of the things he'd broken was the banking laws. There'd been a six-month delay while he and his top team had made the government application to be allowed to operate. But here was the hidden factor. He wanted to go to the board meeting and make his own commitment. He wanted to promise them that the site would be live by the new year. And the way he wanted to get that commitment was to tell them *I* had promised that the site would be live by the new year.

But there was another hidden factor. Despite the football table and pool table and free cereals and fruit for breakfast, despite the cool image, the company was a very macho work environment. Part of the way that the machismo revealed itself was in dramatic shows of "strength." The bosses would show they were in control by ordering a team to work late, or even all through the night, to meet a deadline. Johnny's main competitor at the company — let's call him Frankie — had managed to ruin or substantially damage half a dozen projects using this "management style."

And I knew this. Because I'd worked on a bunch of those projects. They were filled with bad code, written in the middle of the night. And when I'd asked for an explanation of why, somewhere in the answer would be a story about something being done to meet a deadline decreed by Frankie.

Johnny wanted to tell the directors that the project would be done before the new year. And to do that, he needed me to tell him the project would be finished by then. He knew as well as I did that Legal would hold everything up.

But Johnny also wanted to do the whole decreeing the deadline thing. He'd seen his rival Frankie do it — Frankie and Johnny weren't lovers; they were bitter rivals — and he wanted to do it too. He wanted to tell me when the deadline would be. He wanted to see the team working through the night.

It's my fault that this got handled so badly. I should have understood the hidden factor of the board meeting. I should have resisted the panic at being forced to commit to something that I clearly couldn't have committed to. And then I should have asked more questions, to find out what was really going on.

What I should have done is just say "yes" — sure, it will be done before the new year.

And I should have done it with sufficient seriousness, and with an adequately painful look. I should have given him his climactic moment. I should have made a commitment that he knew and I knew neither of us was going to keep. What kind of promise would that be, exactly?
