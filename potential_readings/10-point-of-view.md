# Point of View Is Worth 80 IQ Points

*Source: Introduction ("Why me?"), lines 443–495*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

---

Let's say that you believe me. Let's say you agree that now is a good time to get very good at project managing software development. Why should you listen to me in particular?

Well, there are some obvious, boring reasons. I've been working in the software development industry since 1994. I started out as a developer. I wrote software for oil companies, the military and then a newfangled thing called the internet. Then I worked as a researcher trying to come up with new applications for digital technologies, first for Xerox, then for Cambridge University.

Perhaps a more specific reason why you should listen to me is because I know how to deliver software projects. I've been doing it now for fifteen years. Mostly I've been successful.

Some of these projects made millions for their owners. Some of them weren't about money at all. They were literally about making the world a better place. They helped their users do scientific research, fight Covid-19 and cure cancer. Or they helped the course of criminal justice run more smoothly. I'm not going to say I foiled any gang of criminals single-handed — I didn't — but I did a tiny little bit to help.

I believe that some of those projects wouldn't have done as well if I hadn't been involved. And that's about as much as you can ever say if you're a project manager.

But there's a slightly less boring, less obvious reason. Most people who claim to be experts on project management will try to give you the impression that it is a rational, straightforward thing. They will suggest that if you follow the steps in their methodology, your project will be guaranteed to succeed. I am under no such illusion. I know, and I'm going to show you with multiple examples, that project management is an irrational and emotional business. This, in itself, is a different point of view.

As we'll see, irrationality and emotionality are built into the way projects happen. There will be periods of time in any project where things will be tense and you'll feel bad. That's pretty much guaranteed if you're managing projects. What I try to explain in this book is something that I haven't seen explained in many other places. I want to explain why you're feeling bad, why it's OK to feel bad and what you can best do to deal with these negative feelings.

And I'm going to show you that the way to do this is by seeing project management in a number of different, smart ways. By doing this, we can also avoid the "dumb" ways of seeing. These are the ways that can take away 80 IQ points — that are always present, and are often tempting.
