# Where's Sally's Ball?

*Source: Chapter Some, lines 7356–7445*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

---

In 1985, David Baron-Cohen ran an experiment. His subjects were neurotypical children and autistic children. The results suggest a difference in how they understand a crucial question. The experiment involved a covered basket, a box with a lid, and two fictional children: Sally and Anne.

Sally has a ball. She puts it in her basket — the basket has a cover — and then leaves. Anne takes the ball out of Sally's basket and puts it in her box. The question that the children watching this scene were asked is: "When Sally comes back, where will she look for her ball?"

Neurotypical children understand that Sally will be under the impression that the ball is still in her basket.

Autistic children struggle with this. They often don't get the right answer. They tend to think that Sally will look for the ball in Anne's box. They think that because they know where the ball is, Sally will know the same thing.

In neurotypical adults, this question — at least with balls, baskets and boxes — isn't a challenge. But what about more metaphorical points of view? How do we think about other people thinking about the world? This is far more of a challenge. Part of this challenge is known as the "curse of knowledge." Other people don't know what we know. They don't see the world as we see it. But we are constantly tempted to think that they do.

In a 1990 experiment, psychologist Elisabeth Newton gave subjects a simple task. They were asked to "tap out" the rhythm of popular tunes to an audience of other subjects. For example, "Happy Birthday" might be "ta, tata, ta, ta, taaaa." Then they were asked to estimate the success rate of their communication. How many of the popular tunes that they tapped out would be guessed by their audience?

The average estimate was 50 percent — the tappers imagined that their audience would get the right message with about half of the tunes.

*The real figure was 2.5 percent.*

The tappers overestimated the effectiveness of their communication by a factor of 20.
