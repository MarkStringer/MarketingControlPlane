# Software Is Eating the World

*Source: Introduction ("Why now?"), lines 391–440*
*Estimated reading time: ~2 minutes*

---

You might think: "OK, I get it, seeing things in a different way is useful. But my project has some specific problems. I just want to fix them. I don't have time to kick everything up in the air and philosophize. Maybe we could do this another time?"

Part of my message throughout this book is that if you want to fix your project's immediate problems, now is absolutely the time. Well, for most projects, now is much later than would have been ideal. But now is better than never.

We've needed better ways of thinking about software projects since the 1950s. Humans have been managing the writing of software for at least 75 years, and we're still not very good at it.

And now, more than ever, the ability to write good software is a huge competitive advantage. Customers want software that makes their lives easier and better. They want software that is genuinely valuable to them. They've never needed it more. Being good at software development has become a way of — quite literally — conquering the world.

Am I overstating things? I don't think so. Marc Andreessen wrote the first widely used web browser — Mosaic. Now he runs a hugely influential venture capital firm in Silicon Valley. As he declared more than ten years ago:

> "Software is eating the world."

And since he wrote that, software has increased its appetite. It's taken huge bites out of relationships and dating, politics, social life, music and television. Now, with the arrival of AI, it looks like it's also taking a massive bite out of lots of other, completely different kinds of work. That includes the writing of software itself.

Some companies are good at developing software. Amazon, Facebook and Google are good at it. And some of those companies are now more powerful than some governments.

But this is just one side of the coin. A lot of companies and government organizations are terrible at writing software. Often, the software is paid for and developed, but either it is never used or it never performs as well as promised. Sometimes it even makes things worse.

What if these organizations that are struggling to write good software could be better at it? Maybe they would be just as successful and powerful as companies like Amazon and Facebook. The struggle to deliver good software in a timely manner is one of the biggest problems in the world right now. And it's one that almost nobody is talking about.
