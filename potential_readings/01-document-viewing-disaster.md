# The Document Viewing Disaster

*Source: Chapter Escape, lines 1938–2070*
*Estimated reading time: ~3 minutes*

---

I worked on a project for an organization that was still doing all its business using paper documents. It processed a lot of paper. The project was trying to digitize just one of these paper processes. The aim was to improve it using an electronic document storage system.

The project had a couple of good things going for it. Firstly, it was using an iterative, incremental, Agile approach. Secondly, it had a product owner. She was the person responsible for making decisions about what the product did. And she was a former clerk of the company. That might sound like a junior role, but in this organization, the clerks had clout. She knew all the other clerks and she knew their business very well.

To start with, the project had a tough time. We couldn't get servers. So, we had nowhere to put the software. The open-source document management system that we'd chosen wasn't as mature as we thought. We pushed on through some early design iterations. We dealt with technical problems. Finally, we got to one "show and tell" where the team had working software to show.

It was an odd turning point in the project, because that first demo was so terrible. We tried to demo viewing a collection of documents. The next step was to click on a document title, and the full document was supposed to be displayed.

In that first demo, when we clicked on the document link, a window came up that said, "Do you want to *veiw* your document?" together with "OK" and "Cancel" buttons. Yes, the dialog window had that spelling mistake. When the user clicked "OK" an error message appeared.

The look on the product owner's face! We were about half a million pounds into a two million pound project. And all she had to show was a misspelled dialog box and an error message. It was a hard time for her. It was embarrassing for the team.

Two weeks later it was a slightly different story. Now there was a list of documents. When the document name was clicked, the prompt to open the document had a button that was spelled correctly. Click the button and the document was displayed!

The product owner seemed a bit more relaxed.

Not too many "show and tells" after that, she had a question: "Can I get this on a laptop so I can show it to the clerks?"

Once the software was on her laptop, the product owner went on the road to show it to her colleagues. The demo still wasn't great. It had bugs. But the demo to former workmates went well. She could shrug off any user interface issues or problems. She could give the clerks first sight of something that could make their lives easier.

The product owner came back with a list of problems she found while doing the demo. She also had a list of suggestions for features requested by the clerks. And she had one question: *"When will it be ready?"*

That was the point that the project changed. It wasn't about delivering a list of functions anymore. It was about getting this software out to the clerks. It was about giving them this tiny bit of working software. The path of what we needed to do rolled out before us. And the people we were delivering it to were cheering us on. We'd found our hungry crowd.
