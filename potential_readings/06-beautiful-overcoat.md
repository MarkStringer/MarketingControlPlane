# The Beautiful Overcoat

*Source: Chapter Commit, lines 6158–6210*
*Estimated reading time: ~2 minutes*

---

The writer Alan Bennett tells a story about his mother — Mam. A female friend of the family got a job in the offices of the publisher Faber and Faber. She later married one of the editors, a successful poet.

Mam meets this woman on the street in Leeds. The woman is with her poet husband. Mam describes the gentleman to Bennett. A well-dressed man with an American accent. Bennett realizes that Mam has just met T. S. Eliot — one of the world's most famous poets.

He explains to Mam that the man she met is a very famous poet who won a Nobel prize for literature.

Mam says:

*"I'm not surprised. It was a beautiful overcoat."*

The joke here of course is the idea that the quality of T.S. Eliot's overcoat could have anything to do with him winning a Nobel prize!

What Mam is doing is using quality of clothes as a surrogate. She doesn't understand the quality of poetry. But she knows about overcoats.

We all do this with things that we don't understand. If we are shopping for a used car, should we care about the cleanliness of the interiors? Surely what matters is what's under the bonnet? Cleanliness of the interior isn't a good guide to mechanical condition. Just as the quality of overcoat is no guide to the quality of poet.

So why do used car showrooms put so much emphasis on maintaining the cleanliness of their cars' interiors?

Because evaluating the technical stuff is hard. It is beyond most people's abilities. That doesn't mean they don't know that it's valuable. It does mean that most people don't know how to *evaluate* it. Cleanliness of seats and carpets acts as a surrogate.

On time and to budget, in a project, is being used in the same way as the "lovely overcoat" on the poet and the clean carpets in a used car. It's being used as a surrogate.
